The [KL divergence](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/) measures the gap between two
*different* distributions. Consider now a more local question: if the parameters
are perturbed by an infinitesimal $d\theta$, how much does the distribution itself
move? Expanding the KL between $p_\theta$ and its infinitesimally shifted neighbour
$p_{\theta + d\theta}$, the constant term is zero (a distribution agrees with
itself), the linear term is zero (KL attains its minimum at $d\theta = 0$, so its
gradient there vanishes), and the first surviving term is quadratic:

$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta \,\Vert\, p_{\theta + d\theta}\big)
= \tfrac12\, d\theta^\top F(\theta)\, d\theta + O\big(\lVert d\theta \rVert^3\big).
$$

The matrix $F(\theta)$ appearing in that quadratic form is the **Fisher
information matrix**. It is the local, infinitesimal counterpart of KL; the
[Bregman page](/divergences/bregman/) described a divergence as a
"local-curvature-weighted distance," and $F$ is precisely that curvature expressed
as a matrix. Geometrically it is the **Riemannian metric on the manifold of
distributions**: it measures how far, in distribution-space, a step in
parameter-space actually carries one. Everything on this page follows from this
single idea.

## The score, and why it has zero mean

The central character is the **score** — the gradient of the log-likelihood with
respect to the parameters:

$$
s_\theta(x) = \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x).
$$

It points in the direction that most increases the probability the model assigns to
$x$. Its essential property is that, averaged over data the model itself generates,
it is **zero**:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}\big[\, s_\theta(x) \,\big]
= \int p_\theta\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta
= \int \nabla_\theta\, p_\theta
= \nabla_\theta \!\int p_\theta
= \nabla_\theta 1 = 0.
$$

A well-fit model is, on average, not surprised: there is no systematic direction in
which the data drives the likelihood. Because the mean is zero, the *covariance* of
the score equals the expectation of its outer product, and this is the Fisher
matrix.

## Three faces of one matrix

The Fisher information can be written in three equivalent ways:

$$
F(\theta)
= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}\!\big[\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta\; \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta^{\top} \,\big]}_{\text{(1) covariance of the score}}
= \underbrace{-\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}\!\big[\, \nabla_\theta^2 \log p_\theta \,\big]}_{\text{(2) negative expected Hessian}}
= \underbrace{\nabla_{\theta'}^2\, \mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta \,\Vert\, p_{\theta'}\big)\big|_{\theta' = \theta}}_{\text{(3) curvature of KL}}.
$$

These are three perspectives on the same object:

| form | reads as | relevant to |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| (1) score covariance | how the gradient *varies* across samples | estimation, sampling, an outer-product estimate of $F$ |
| (2) negative expected Hessian | the average *curvature* of the log-likelihood | optimization — $F$ as a stand-in for the loss Hessian |
| (3) Hessian of KL | how fast the *distribution* changes per step | geometry, trust regions, the natural gradient |

The equality of (1) and (2) — the **information matrix equality** — is what
distinguishes $F$: a quantity built from *first* derivatives (squared gradients)
equals one built from *second* derivatives (a Hessian). It holds under the model,
in expectation over $y \sim p_\theta$.

<Aside type="caution" title="The empirical Fisher is not the Fisher">
	Replacing the expectation over model-drawn labels with the *observed* labels in
	the dataset yields the **empirical Fisher** — less expensive, since those labels
	are already available, and the quantity that optimizers such as Adam loosely
	approximate. It equals the true $F$ only at the optimum of a well-specified
	model, however; away from there the two can differ substantially. When a paper
	refers to the "Fisher," one should determine which of the two is intended.
</Aside>

## What the term "information" denotes

The name is literal. The **Cramér–Rao bound** states that for *any* unbiased
estimator $\hat\theta$ built from data,

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta) \;\succeq\; F(\theta)^{-1}.
$$

The inverse Fisher is a lower bound on how precisely the parameters can be
determined. A large $F$ corresponds to a sharply peaked likelihood — the data
localizes $\theta$ tightly, so the best achievable error is small. A flat direction
in $F$ is a direction the data barely constrains. The information is thus the
inverse of the unavoidable variance.

## A network, neuron by neuron

To make the construction concrete, we now compute $F$ for an explicit network.
Consider a small binary classifier: two inputs $x = (x_1, x_2)$, two sigmoid
hidden units, and one sigmoid output.

$$
z_k = w_{k1} x_1 + w_{k2} x_2 + b_k, \quad a_k = \sigma(z_k), \quad k \in \{1, 2\};
\qquad
u = v_1 a_1 + v_2 a_2 + c, \quad p = \sigma(u).
$$

The output is a Bernoulli variable with bias $p$, so the likelihood is Bernoulli
and the loss is the negative log-likelihood
$\ell = -\big[\, y \ln p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - p)\,\big]$. The entire calculation rests
on a single fact about the output logit $u$:

$$
\frac{\partial \log p_\theta(y \mid x)}{\partial u} = y - p.
$$

Every parameter influences the loss *only through* $u$, so by the chain rule its
score factorizes into the shared residual $(y - p)$ multiplied by a
parameter-specific **sensitivity** $g_\alpha = \partial u / \partial \theta_\alpha$:

$$
\frac{\partial \log p_\theta}{\partial \theta_\alpha} = (y - p)\, g_\alpha(x).
$$

These $g_\alpha$ are precisely the quantities that [backpropagation](/neural-networks/backpropagation/)
already computes. Consider now the Fisher entry. Because the only randomness in the
score (at fixed $x$) is the label, and
$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p}\big[(y - p)^2\big] = p(1 - p)$ is the Bernoulli variance,
every entry reduces to

$$
F_{\alpha\beta}
= \mathbb{E}_x\Big[\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_\theta}\big[(y - p)^2\big]\, g_\alpha\, g_\beta \,\Big]
= \mathbb{E}_x\big[\, p(1 - p)\, g_\alpha(x)\, g_\beta(x) \,\big].
$$

The entire matrix is thus a single expectation of a weighted outer product. Writing
$\sigma'(z_k) = a_k(1 - a_k)$, the following table gives every parameter of the
network together with its score, its sensitivity, and its **diagonal Fisher
entry** — the exact formulas:

| neuron | parameter $\theta_\alpha$ | score $\partial_\alpha \log p_\theta$ | sensitivity $g_\alpha = \partial u / \partial \theta_\alpha$ | Fisher diagonal $F_{\alpha\alpha} = \mathbb{E}_x[\, p(1-p)\, g_\alpha^2\,]$ |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **output** | $c$ | $(y - p)$ | $1$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p) \,]$ |
| | $v_1$ | $(y - p)\, a_1$ | $a_1$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, a_1^2 \,]$ |
| | $v_2$ | $(y - p)\, a_2$ | $a_2$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, a_2^2 \,]$ |
| **hidden 1** | $b_1$ | $(y - p)\, v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)$ | $v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, v_1^2 a_1^2(1{-}a_1)^2 \,]$ |
| | $w_{11}$ | $(y - p)\, v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)\, x_1$ | $v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)\, x_1$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, v_1^2 a_1^2(1{-}a_1)^2 x_1^2 \,]$ |
| | $w_{12}$ | $(y - p)\, v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)\, x_2$ | $v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)\, x_2$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, v_1^2 a_1^2(1{-}a_1)^2 x_2^2 \,]$ |
| **hidden 2** | $b_2$ | $(y - p)\, v_2 a_2(1{-}a_2)$ | $v_2 a_2(1{-}a_2)$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, v_2^2 a_2^2(1{-}a_2)^2 \,]$ |
| | $w_{21}$ | $(y - p)\, v_2 a_2(1{-}a_2)\, x_1$ | $v_2 a_2(1{-}a_2)\, x_1$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, v_2^2 a_2^2(1{-}a_2)^2 x_1^2 \,]$ |
| | $w_{22}$ | $(y - p)\, v_2 a_2(1{-}a_2)\, x_2$ | $v_2 a_2(1{-}a_2)\, x_2$ | $\mathbb{E}[\, p(1-p)\, v_2^2 a_2^2(1{-}a_2)^2 x_2^2 \,]$ |

Off-diagonal entries are the same expectation with two *different* sensitivities;
for example, the coupling between the output bias and a first-layer weight is
$F_{c,\, w_{11}} = \mathbb{E}_x\big[\, p(1-p)\, (1)\,(v_1 a_1(1{-}a_1)\, x_1) \,\big]$.
Collecting all nine sensitivities into one vector $g(x)$, the full matrix is

$$
F = \mathbb{E}_x\big[\, p(1 - p)\, g(x)\, g(x)^{\top} \,\big].
$$

Several conclusions may be drawn from the table. The output bias $c$ carries the
most information ($g_c = 1$, never attenuated). A first-layer weight's information
is scaled by $v_k^2 a_k^2(1{-}a_k)^2 x_j^2$, and it **vanishes when the hidden unit
saturates** ($a_k \to 0$ or $1$, so $a_k(1{-}a_k) \to 0$), when its outgoing weight
$v_k$ is small, or when its input $x_j$ is negligible. Moreover, for a single
example $p(1-p)\,g g^\top$ is **rank one**: one data point informs the parameters
along exactly one direction. Information accumulates only as one averages over
data.

## End to end: how the information flows

The Bernoulli case is one instance of a pattern that holds for any network whose
output parameterizes an exponential-family likelihood. Let $f_\theta(x)$ denote the
network's output (here the scalar logit $u$; for a classifier, the logit vector),
and $J_\theta(x) = \partial f_\theta / \partial \theta$ its Jacobian. Then

$$
F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x\big[\, J_\theta(x)^{\top}\, \Lambda(x)\, J_\theta(x) \,\big],
\qquad
\Lambda(x) = \operatorname{Cov}_{y \sim p_\theta}\big[\, t(y) \,\big],
$$

where $\Lambda$ is the **Fisher information in output space** — the curvature of the
NLL measured in the output's own coordinates. It depends only on the output head:

| output head | likelihood | output metric $\Lambda$ |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| linear (identity) | Gaussian | $\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\, I$ |
| binary (sigmoid) | Bernoulli | $p(1 - p)$ |
| multiclass (softmax) | Categorical | $\operatorname{diag}(p) - p\, p^{\top}$ |

This $F = \mathbb{E}[J^\top \Lambda J]$ is exactly the **Generalized Gauss–Newton**
matrix: when the loss is the NLL that matches the output head, the Fisher matrix
*equals* the Gauss–Newton approximation of the loss Hessian. The information flows
in three stages, from output to input:

$$
\underbrace{\Lambda}_{\text{born at the output}}
\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{carried back through } J\ \text{(the backprop pass)}\;}\;
\underbrace{J^{\top} \Lambda J}_{\text{per example}}
\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathbb{E}_x\;}\;
F
\;\xrightarrow{\;F^{-1}\;}\;
\text{natural step}.
$$

1. **It originates at the output.** $\Lambda$ specifies how sharply the
   log-likelihood bends per unit change in the network's prediction. A *confident*
   sigmoid ($p \to 0$ or $1$) has $p(1-p) \to 0$: a sure prediction carries almost
   no information, because little remains to be learned from it. Uncertainty
   ($p = \tfrac12$) is where the information is richest.
2. **It is carried backward by the Jacobian.** The same reverse-mode pass that
   backprop uses to propagate gradients propagates $\Lambda$ back through $J$. Where
   a unit is saturated, $\partial a_k / \partial z_k \to 0$ restricts the flow; this
   is the vanishing-gradient problem, reinterpreted as the statement that
   information cannot pass through a saturated neuron.
3. **It accumulates into $F$.** Averaged over data as $J^\top \Lambda J$, the
   output-space curvature is *pulled back* into a metric on parameter space. $F$ is
   the distribution's geometry, expressed in the network's own coordinates.

## Why it is a superior gradient

The raw gradient $\nabla_\theta L$ is the steepest-descent direction in **Euclidean
parameter space**; it treats $\theta$ as if a step of size $1$ meant the same thing
in every coordinate. Parameter space, however, is an arbitrary chart. Rescaling a
layer or replacing $\sigma$ with $\log\sigma$ causes the gradient to point in a
different direction, even though the *model* is unchanged. Plain SGD's trajectory
therefore depends on a choice of coordinates unrelated to the distributions.

The **natural gradient** addresses this by measuring steepest descent in
*distribution-space*, under the Fisher metric:

$$
\tilde\nabla_\theta L = F(\theta)^{-1}\, \nabla_\theta L.
$$

It is **invariant to reparameterization**: it changes the *distribution* by a fixed
amount per step, irrespective of the chosen coordinates for $\theta$. The Gaussian
$\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)$ illustrates the effect concretely. Its Fisher matrix is
$F = \operatorname{diag}(1/\sigma^2,\; 2/\sigma^2)$, so $F^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2,\; \sigma^2/2)$,
and the $\mu$-component of the natural gradient becomes

$$
\tilde\nabla_\mu L = \sigma^2 \cdot \frac{\mu - \mu^\star}{\sigma^2} = \mu - \mu^\star.
$$

The $\sigma^2$ in $F^{-1}$ exactly cancels the $1/\sigma^2$ that the raw
$\mu$-gradient carries. When $\sigma$ is small, the raw gradient encounters a
$\mu$-slope that grows like $1/\sigma^2$ and takes an excessively large step,
whereas the natural gradient takes the same measured $(\mu - \mu^\star)$ step at
every scale. The figure below compares them — vanilla GD in amber, natural GD in
teal, both minimizing the KL to a fixed target $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ (the target
marked at the center):

The faint ellipses are the Fisher metric's "unit balls" — the sets of parameter
steps that all change the distribution by the same KL amount. They are **small
where $\sigma$ is small** (at low $\sigma$, a small parameter move is a large change
in distribution) and large at high $\sigma$. Vanilla GD disregards them: from a
small-$\sigma$ start it overshoots severely, traversing the surface erratically,
whereas natural GD respects the ellipses and proceeds cautiously precisely where it
should, moving directly to the target. Setting the start to "wide $\sigma$" reveals
the opposite failure: vanilla GD now *advances slowly*, because at high $\sigma$ its
$\mu$-gradient is small.

<Aside type="note" title="This is the step a trust region takes">
	A fixed-size step in the Fisher metric *is* a fixed-KL step — a constant Fisher
	ellipse. This is precisely what [TRPO](/reinforcement-learning/trust-regions/)
	requires when it bounds how far the policy may move: its update
	$\theta \leftarrow \theta + \sqrt{2\delta / (g^\top F^{-1} g)}\; F^{-1} g$ is a
	natural-gradient step, scaled to land on the boundary of a KL trust region of
	size $\delta$. The Fisher matrix is the link between a small step in behavior and
	the corresponding direction in which to move the weights.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="Why you rarely invert F exactly">
	$F$ is $d \times d$ for $d$ parameters, so inverting it costs $O(d^3)$, which is
	infeasible when $d$ reaches the millions. In practice the natural gradient is
	therefore always an approximation: a *diagonal* or empirical Fisher (the
	[Adam / RMSprop](/neural-networks/optimizers/) family is a coarse relative),
	**K-FAC**'s Kronecker-factored blocks, or — as in TRPO — solving $F x = g$ with
	conjugate gradients using only Fisher-vector products, without ever forming $F$.
</Aside>

## Verification in code

The following first builds the exact 2-2-1 network above and assembles its Fisher
matrix in two independent ways: the closed form
$\mathbb{E}_x[\,p(1-p)\, g g^\top\,]$, and the raw definition — sampling labels
$y \sim p_\theta$ from the *model* and averaging the outer products of the scores.
The two must agree, and the heatmap shows the information concentrating at the
output neuron:

<PyCell
	client:only="react"
	id="div-fisher-network"
	title="fisher_network.py"
	code={`import numpy as np
np.random.seed(0)
sig = lambda z: 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))

# a tiny 2 -> 2 (sigmoid) -> 1 (sigmoid) classifier, fixed random weights
W = np.array([[0.9, -0.4], [0.3, 0.8]])    # W[k,j]: into hidden k from input j
b = np.array([0.1, -0.2])
v = np.array([1.1, -0.7])                  # output weights from hidden a_k
c = 0.05
names = ["w11", "w12", "w21", "w22", "b1", "b2", "v1", "v2", "c"]

def sens(x):
    # g_alpha = du/dtheta_alpha -- exactly the backprop sensitivities of logit u
    z = W @ x + b
    a = sig(z)
    ap = a * (1 - a)                       # sigma'(z_k) = a_k (1 - a_k)
    gw = np.array([v[0]*ap[0]*x[0], v[0]*ap[0]*x[1],
                   v[1]*ap[1]*x[0], v[1]*ap[1]*x[1]])
    gb = np.array([v[0]*ap[0], v[1]*ap[1]])
    gv = a.copy()
    gc = np.array([1.0])
    return np.concatenate([gw, gb, gv, gc]), sig(v @ a + c)

X = np.random.randn(40000, 2)              # data distribution over inputs

# (1) closed form  F = E_x[ p(1-p) g g^T ]
F1 = np.zeros((9, 9))
for x in X:
    g, p = sens(x)
    F1 += p * (1 - p) * np.outer(g, g)
F1 /= len(X)

# (2) by definition: sample y ~ p_theta (the MODEL), average score outer products
F2 = np.zeros((9, 9))
for x in X:
    g, p = sens(x)
    y = 1.0 if np.random.rand() < p else 0.0
    s = (y - p) * g                        # the score
    F2 += np.outer(s, s)
F2 /= len(X)

print("max |F_closed - F_sampled| =", round(np.max(np.abs(F1 - F2)), 5), " (-> 0)")
print()
print("diagonal Fisher entry per parameter  F_aa = E[p(1-p) g_a^2]:")
for nm, d in zip(names, np.diag(F1)):
    print("  " + nm.ljust(4), round(float(d), 4))

g0, p0 = sens(X[0])
print()
print("rank of one example's p(1-p) g g^T :", np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.outer(g0, g0)), "(always 1)")

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.7, 4.1))
im = ax.imshow(F1, cmap="magma")
ax.set_xticks(range(9)); ax.set_yticks(range(9))
ax.set_xticklabels(names, rotation=45, fontsize=8); ax.set_yticklabels(names, fontsize=8)
ax.set_title("Fisher information  F = E[p(1-p) g g^T]", fontsize=9)
fig.colorbar(im, fraction=0.046)
fig.tight_layout()
`}

$\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)$ to a target by forward KL and runs plain gradient descent
against the $F^{-1}$-preconditioned step from the same start, at the same learning
rate:

	code={`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Fit N(mu, sigma) to the target N(0, 1) by forward KL.
mt, st = 0.0, 1.0
def loss(mu, s):
    A = st**2 + (mt - mu)**2
    return np.log(s / st) + A / (2 * s**2) - 0.5
def grad(mu, s):                                  # raw gradient of the KL loss
    A = st**2 + (mt - mu)**2
    return np.array([(mu - mt) / s**2, 1.0 / s - A / s**3])
def natural(mu, s):                               # F^{-1} grad, F = diag(1/s^2, 2/s^2)
    A = st**2 + (mt - mu)**2
    return np.array([mu - mt, s / 2 - A / (2 * s)])

def run(step_fn, lr=0.06, n=120, start=(-2.1, 0.4)):
    p = np.array(start, float); path = [p.copy()]
    for _ in range(n):
        p = p - lr * step_fn(*p)
        p[0] = min(3.0, max(-3.0, p[0]))          # keep inside the plotting box
        p[1] = min(3.0, max(0.05, p[1]))
        path.append(p.copy())
    return np.array(path)

van, nat = run(grad), run(natural)
lv = np.array([loss(*q) for q in van])
ln = np.array([loss(*q) for q in nat])
hit = lambda L: next((i for i, x in enumerate(L) if x < 0.05), len(L))
print("steps to reach KL < 0.05    vanilla:", hit(lv), "  natural:", hit(ln))
print("final KL after 120 steps    vanilla:", round(float(lv[-1]), 4), "  natural:", round(float(ln[-1]), 4))

mus = np.linspace(-3, 3, 240); sigs = np.linspace(0.1, 3, 240)
MU, SG = np.meshgrid(mus, sigs)
Z = np.minimum(6, loss(MU, SG))

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 3.6))
axL.contourf(MU, SG, Z, levels=24, cmap="Greys")
axL.plot(van[:, 0], van[:, 1], "-o", ms=2.5, lw=1.4, color="#e0b15e", label="vanilla GD")
axL.plot(nat[:, 0], nat[:, 1], "-o", ms=2.5, lw=1.4, color="#13a89a", label="natural GD")
axL.plot(0, 1, "w*", ms=13)
axL.set_xlabel("mu"); axL.set_ylabel("sigma")
axL.set_title("path on the KL loss surface", fontsize=9); axL.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8)

axR.plot(lv, lw=1.6, color="#e0b15e", label="vanilla GD")
axR.plot(ln, lw=1.6, color="#13a89a", label="natural GD")
axR.set_yscale("log"); axR.set_xlabel("step"); axR.set_ylabel("KL loss (log)")
axR.set_title("natural gradient converges far faster", fontsize=9)
axR.legend(fontsize=8); axR.grid(alpha=0.3)
fig.tight_layout()

## Where this connects

The Fisher matrix is the connection between this track and optimization. It
underlies the [Bregman](/divergences/bregman/) and
[KL](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/) divergences whose curvature it *is*. Beyond
it, the natural gradient is the basis of the **natural policy gradient** and
[TRPO](/reinforcement-learning/trust-regions/), where a KL trust region prevents a
policy from destabilizing itself; and its diagonal, less expensive variants are
what the [Adam family](/neural-networks/optimizers/) of optimizers approximate
whenever they rescale a gradient by its own running variance. A single matrix — the
curvature of KL — proves to be the appropriate way to measure distance for nearly
everything that learns a distribution.
