Consider the question of how far one distribution lies from another. In many
settings a true *distance* is not what is required; what is required instead is a
**divergence**: a number that is zero when the two distributions agree and grows
as they separate, yet which is permitted to break the rules a metric must obey.

A divergence $D(p \,\|\, q)$ is required to satisfy only two conditions:

$$
D(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0, \qquad D(p \,\|\, q) = 0 \iff p = q.
$$

It is non-negative, and it is zero precisely when its arguments coincide. These
two conditions constitute the entire contract.

## Not a distance

A metric $d$ guarantees two further properties that a divergence does **not**:

- **Symmetry.** A metric guarantees $d(p, q) = d(q, p)$. A divergence does not:
  in general $D(p \,\|\, q) \neq D(q \,\|\, p)$. For this reason the two
  arguments are written with a bar $\|$ between them and never a comma; the
  notation serves as a standing reminder that *order matters*.
- **The triangle inequality.** A metric guarantees $d(p, r) \le d(p, q) + d(q,
  r)$. Divergences routinely violate it, so proximity to a third distribution
  conveys little.

These two metric axioms are precisely what impose a *geometry* of straight lines
and shortest paths, and the natural ways to compare distributions — information
lost, likelihood assigned, surprise incurred — are not symmetric. Encoding a
message built for $q$ using the true distribution $p$ costs a different number of
bits than the reverse. The divergence faithfully reflects this asymmetry, whereas
insisting on symmetry would discard it.

<Aside type="note" title="Reading the bar">
	$D(p \,\|\, q)$ is read "the divergence **from** $q$ **to** $p$", or "of $p$
	with respect to $q$". By near-universal convention the first slot holds the
	*reference* or *true* distribution and the second the *approximation*. Because
	conventions vary, one should always confirm which argument occupies which slot.
</Aside>

## The role of divergences in machine learning

Divergences serve as the loss functions of probabilistic modeling. Nearly every
time a model is fit to data or to another model, some divergence is being
minimized:

- **Maximum likelihood** minimizes a *forward* KL between the data and the model.
- **Variational inference** and the ELBO minimize a *reverse* KL.
- **Mirror descent** and exponential-family clustering are built on **Bregman**
  divergences.
- **GANs** and many two-sample tests are framed through **f-divergences**.

Once the divergence — and its *direction* — has been chosen correctly, the
learning algorithm frequently follows in a nearly mechanical fashion.

## Two families

Almost every divergence encountered in practice belongs to one of two families,
each generated from a single convex function:

| family | built from | examples |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Bregman** | a convex potential $\varphi$, via its tangent | squared error, KL, Itakura–Saito, Mahalanobis |
| **f-divergence** | a convex $f$ applied to the ratio $p/q$ | KL, total variation, $\chi^2$, Hellinger |

The **KL divergence belongs to both families** — it is the Bregman
divergence of negative entropy *and* the f-divergence of $f(t) = t\ln t$. This
dual membership, together with its asymmetry, makes it the natural starting
point for closer study.
