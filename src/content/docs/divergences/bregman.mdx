Any strictly convex, differentiable function $\varphi$ determines a divergence.
The **Bregman divergence** generated by $\varphi$ is the error incurred by
approximating $\varphi$ near $q$ with its tangent and then evaluating at $p$:

$$
D_\varphi(p \,\|\, q) = \varphi(p) - \varphi(q) - \langle \nabla\varphi(q),\, p - q \rangle.
$$

The right-hand side is the *(actual value of $\varphi$ at $p$)* minus the
*(first-order Taylor estimate of $\varphi$ at $p$, constructed at $q$)*. Because
$\varphi$ is convex, its graph lies **above** every tangent, so this gap is never
negative, and it closes to zero only when $p = q$. The two divergence axioms
therefore follow directly from convexity.

Three observations are worth drawing out from the figure:

- **It is a gap, and is therefore non-negative.** The curve never falls below its
  tangent; the teal segment can shrink to nothing (at $p = q$) but never changes
  sign.
- **Curvature governs the magnitude.** Where $\varphi$ bends sharply, a small step
  from $q$ to $p$ opens a large gap; on a nearly flat stretch the same step
  produces almost no gap. The divergence is thus a *local-curvature-weighted*
  notion of distance.
- **Order matters.** Interchanging $p$ and $q$ changes the value, and the readout
  reports both. The sole exception is $\varphi(x) = \tfrac12 x^2$, whose constant
  curvature renders $D_\varphi(p \,\|\, q) = \tfrac12 (p-q)^2$ exactly symmetric.

## The generator picks the divergence

Different potentials $\varphi$ produce divergences already known under other
names. Several standard cases follow (here $\nabla\varphi$ is taken
componentwise):

| generator $\varphi(x)$ | $D_\varphi(p \,\|\, q)$ | name |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| $\tfrac12 \lVert x \rVert^2$ | $\tfrac12 \lVert p - q \rVert^2$ | squared Euclidean |
| $\sum_i x_i \ln x_i$ | $\sum_i p_i \ln\frac{p_i}{q_i} - \sum_i p_i + \sum_i q_i$ | generalized KL |
| $-\sum_i \ln x_i$ | $\sum_i \big(\frac{p_i}{q_i} - \ln\frac{p_i}{q_i} - 1\big)$ | Itakura–Saito |
| $\tfrac12\, x^\top A x$ | $\tfrac12 (p-q)^\top A (p-q)$ | Mahalanobis |

The second row merits particular attention: **negative entropy generates KL**. On
the probability simplex the sums $\sum p_i$ and $\sum q_i$ both equal $1$ and
cancel, leaving exactly $\sum_i p_i \ln(p_i/q_i)$ — the KL divergence treated on
the [next page](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/).

## Properties worth knowing

- **Convex in the first argument** $p$ (it inherits the convexity of $\varphi$),
  though *not* in general convex in $q$.
- **Asymmetric**, with no triangle inequality; a Bregman divergence is therefore
  not a metric, except in the squared-Euclidean special case.
- **The mean is the Bregman centroid.** For *any* generator $\varphi$,
  $\;\arg\min_c \sum_i D_\varphi(x_i \,\|\, c) = \frac1n \sum_i x_i$. The ordinary
  average is thus the correct "center" under every Bregman divergence
  simultaneously, a property that underlies k-means and its exponential-family
  generalizations.

## Verification in code

The geometric definition and the closed forms in the table should agree to the
last digit. The following computes the Bregman divergence directly from the
definition — $\varphi(p) - \varphi(q) - \nabla\varphi(q)\cdot(p-q)$ — for negative
entropy and compares it against KL. The arguments are then interchanged to exhibit
the asymmetry:

	title="bregman.py"
	code={`import numpy as np

# Bregman divergence straight from the definition, for any convex phi.
def bregman(phi, grad_phi, p, q):
    p, q = np.asarray(p, float), np.asarray(q, float)
    return phi(p) - phi(q) - grad_phi(q) @ (p - q)

# Negative entropy and its gradient.
phi      = lambda x: np.sum(x * np.log(x))
grad_phi = lambda x: np.log(x) + 1.0

# Two distributions on a 3-outcome simplex (each sums to 1).
p = np.array([0.70, 0.20, 0.10])
q = np.array([0.30, 0.30, 0.40])

kl = lambda a, b: np.sum(a * np.log(a / b))

print("Bregman(p || q) =", round(bregman(phi, grad_phi, p, q), 6))
print("KL(p || q)      =", round(kl(p, q),                      6), " <- identical")
print()
print("asymmetry — same two distributions, swapped:")
print("  D(p || q) =", round(bregman(phi, grad_phi, p, q), 4))
print("  D(q || p) =", round(bregman(phi, grad_phi, q, p), 4), " <- different number")
`}
/>

<Aside type="tip" title="Try it">
	Substituting the squared-error generator — `phi = lambda x: 0.5 * x @ x` with
	`grad_phi = lambda x: x` — and arbitrary vectors `p`, `q` yields equal values
	for `D(p||q)` and `D(q||p)`: this is the one symmetric Bregman divergence.
</Aside>
