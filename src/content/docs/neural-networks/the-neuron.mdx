A neuron is among the simplest units of computation. It takes a vector of inputs
$\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, multiplies each by a **weight**, adds a
**bias**, and passes the result through a nonlinear **activation** $\sigma$.

$$
z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b,
\qquad
a = \sigma(z)
$$

The pre-activation $z$ is an affine function of the inputs — a line, a plane, a
hyperplane. A neuron's expressive power beyond a straight boundary derives
entirely from the activation $\sigma$.

## An interactive single neuron

Consider a single neuron with two inputs. The square renders its output $a$ across
the **entire input plane** $(x_1, x_2)$ — dark denotes low values, light denotes
high values — and the teal contour marks the **decision boundary**, where the
output crosses its middle value. The controls below vary the weights and bias; the
panel renders the neuron's output across the input plane.

Several properties are evident as the controls are varied:

- **The weights set the boundary's orientation.** The boundary is always
  perpendicular to the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2)$.
- **The bias translates the boundary.** Changing $b$ slides the boundary without
  rotating it.
- **The activation sets the softness.** `sigmoid` and `tanh` fade gradually
  across the boundary, whereas `ReLU` is flat-then-linear.

## The role of the nonlinearity

Without $\sigma$, composing neurons would yield no additional expressiveness: a
composition of affine maps is itself affine. Formally, if $f(\mathbf{x}) =
W_2(W_1 \mathbf{x} + b_1) + b_2$, then $f(\mathbf{x}) = W' \mathbf{x} + b'$ for
some single $W', b'$. It is the nonlinearity that allows depth to confer
expressiveness.
