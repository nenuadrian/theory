Gradient descent uses a single piece of local information: the gradient $\nabla
f$, the direction of steepest ascent. That is the **first-order** picture. But a
loss surface also *curves*, and how it curves — captured by the **Hessian** and,
beyond it, the **third-derivative tensor** — determines how the gradient itself
changes from step to step, how quickly descent converges, and whether a critical
point is a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle.

These objects are precisely the successive terms of the **Taylor expansion** about
a point $p$:

$$
f(p + \Delta)
= f(p)
+ \underbrace{\nabla f(p)^\top \Delta}_{\text{1st: gradient}}
+ \underbrace{\tfrac12\, \Delta^\top H(p)\, \Delta}_{\text{2nd: Hessian}}
+ \underbrace{\tfrac16 \textstyle\sum_{i,j,k} \mathcal{T}_{ijk}\, \Delta_i \Delta_j \Delta_k}_{\text{3rd: tensor}}
+ O(\|\Delta\|^4).
$$

The gradient is a vector, $\nabla f \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The Hessian is the matrix of
second partials, $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$. The
third-order object $\mathcal{T}_{ijk} = \partial^3 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j
\partial x_k$ is a rank-3 **tensor** — it records how the curvature itself bends.
Each term is one order more expensive and one order more accurate.

## First and second order: the gradient and the Hessian

The field below is the surface $f(x, y) = \sin x \cdot \cos y$, viewed from above
as filled contours. Drag the point. The teal arrow is the gradient $\nabla f$. The
cross and ellipse are the Hessian, displayed through its **eigendecomposition**
$H = \sum_i \lambda_i\, u_i u_i^\top$: the eigenvectors $u_i$ are the principal
curvature directions, and the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ are the curvatures along
them — drawn teal where positive (the surface curves upward, a bowl) and pink
where negative (a dome).

<CurvatureField client:visible />

Three readings of the Hessian follow directly:

- **Definiteness classifies the point.** Both eigenvalues positive ($H \succ 0$) is
  a convex bowl; both negative, a concave dome; mixed signs, a **saddle**. At a
  critical point ($\nabla f = 0$) this is the second-derivative test.
- **The condition number $\kappa = |\lambda_{\max}| / |\lambda_{\min}|$** measures
  anisotropy — how elongated the ellipse is. Large $\kappa$ is an ill-conditioned
  ravine, and it is exactly what makes plain gradient descent zig-zag, since a
  single scalar step size cannot suit both a steep and a shallow direction at once.
- **Newton's method rescales by curvature.** Minimizing the quadratic model
  $m(\Delta) = f + \nabla f^\top \Delta + \tfrac12 \Delta^\top H \Delta$ gives the
  step $\Delta^\star = -H^{-1} \nabla f$ (violet). Where the bowl is well-behaved it
  steps straight to the bottom, in one move, regardless of conditioning — whereas
  the plain gradient step $-\nabla f$ (dashed) merely points downhill.

The same operator that helps near a minimum is treacherous elsewhere: $-H^{-1}
\nabla f$ is attracted to *any* critical point, saddles and maxima included. In the
high-dimensional non-convex landscapes of deep networks, saddle points vastly
outnumber minima, which is why naive second-order steps must be modified before
they can be trusted (Dauphin et al., 2014).

## The Taylor tower in three dimensions

The Hessian is the second rung of a ladder. The surface below renders all three
approximations at once: the **tangent plane** (order 1, the gradient), the
**tangent paraboloid** (order 2, the Hessian), and the **cubic** correction
(order 3, the tensor $\mathcal{T}$). Toggle each, grow the patch radius, and move
the expansion point.

<TaylorSurface3D client:only="react" />

Two behaviors are worth provoking. First, as the patch grows the plane peels away
from the surface first, the paraboloid next, the cubic last: the remainder of a
$k$-th order model is $O(\|\Delta\|^{k+1})$, reported live as the RMS error — each
order is roughly one power of $r$ tighter. Second, select the **monkey saddle**
$x^3 - 3xy^2$ and sit at the origin: there $\nabla f = 0$ *and* $H = 0$, so the
plane and the paraboloid are both perfectly flat and reconstruct nothing. Only the
third-order term carries the three-fold shape — a case where curvature is silent
and the leading geometry is entirely third order.

<Aside type="note" title="Why we usually stop at second order">
	For $n$ parameters the gradient has $n$ entries, the Hessian $n^2$, and the
	third-order tensor $n^3$. For a network with millions of parameters even forming
	$H$ is out of reach, let alone $\mathcal{T}$. Practical second-order methods never
	build the Hessian explicitly: they use **Hessian–vector products** $H v$, computed
	at the cost of a few gradients (Pearlmutter, 1994), inside truncated-Newton or
	**Hessian-free** schemes (Martens, 2010). The third-order tensor is essentially
	never used directly — it appears here to make the structure of the Taylor series
	concrete.
</Aside>

## In code

The following cell computes the gradient, Hessian, and full third-order tensor of
$f(x, y) = \sin x \cos y$ from their closed forms, checks the tensor against a
finite-difference estimate, and then measures the RMS error of each Taylor model
over a shrinking patch. The fitted log–log slopes recover the theoretical orders
$k + 1$ almost exactly:

	id="curv-taylor"
	title="taylor_orders.py"
	code={`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# f(x, y) = sin(x) cos(y) — exact derivatives to third order
def f(x, y): return np.sin(x) * np.cos(y)

def gradient(x, y):
    return np.array([np.cos(x) * np.cos(y), -np.sin(x) * np.sin(y)])

def hessian(x, y):
    return np.array([[-np.sin(x) * np.cos(y), -np.cos(x) * np.sin(y)],
                     [-np.cos(x) * np.sin(y), -np.sin(x) * np.cos(y)]])

# third-order tensor T[i,j,k] = d3f / dxi dxj dxk  (2x2x2, fully symmetric)
def tensor3(x, y):
    fxxx = -np.cos(x) * np.cos(y)
    fxxy =  np.sin(x) * np.sin(y)
    fxyy = -np.cos(x) * np.cos(y)
    fyyy =  np.sin(x) * np.sin(y)
    T = np.zeros((2, 2, 2))
    T[0,0,0] = fxxx
    T[0,0,1] = T[0,1,0] = T[1,0,0] = fxxy
    T[0,1,1] = T[1,0,1] = T[1,1,0] = fxyy
    T[1,1,1] = fyyy
    return T

p = np.array([0.9, 0.6])
g, H, T = gradient(*p), hessian(*p), tensor3(*p)

# check the tensor by numerically differentiating the Hessian
h, e = 1e-4, np.eye(2)
Tnum = np.zeros((2, 2, 2))
for k in range(2):
    Tnum[:, :, k] = (hessian(*(p + h*e[k])) - hessian(*(p - h*e[k]))) / (2*h)

print("point      ", p.tolist())
print("gradient   ", np.round(g, 4).tolist())
print("Hessian    ", np.round(H, 4).tolist())
print("3rd tensor ", np.round(T, 4).tolist())
print("tensor FD check, max error =", float(np.abs(T - Tnum).max()))

# k-th order Taylor model about p, at offset d
def taylor(d, k):
    z = f(*p)
    if k >= 1: z = z + g @ d
    if k >= 2: z = z + 0.5 * d @ H @ d
    if k >= 3: z = z + (T * d[:,None,None] * d[None,:,None] * d[None,None,:]).sum() / 6
    return z

# RMS remainder over a ring of radius r; slope of log-error vs log-r is k+1
radii = np.logspace(-1.4, 0.0, 12)
rms = {k: [] for k in (1, 2, 3)}
for r in radii:
    th = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 60)
    ring = np.stack([r*np.cos(th), r*np.sin(th)], 1)
    for k in (1, 2, 3):
        err = [f(*(p + d)) - taylor(d, k) for d in ring]
        rms[k].append(np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(err))))

print()
print("order   fitted slope   (theory k+1)")
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    s = np.polyfit(np.log(radii), np.log(rms[k]), 1)[0]
    print("  " + str(k) + "         " + str(round(float(s), 2)) + "            " + str(k + 1))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 3.6))
for k, c in zip((1, 2, 3), ("#2aa198", "#b8860b", "#7a5cc8")):
    ax.loglog(radii, rms[k], "o-", color=c, label="order " + str(k))
ax.set_xlabel("patch radius r"); ax.set_ylabel("RMS Taylor error")
ax.set_title("a k-th order model has O(r^(k+1)) error")
ax.grid(True, which="both", alpha=0.25); ax.legend()
fig.tight_layout()
`}

<Aside type="tip" title="Connecting back to optimizers">
	The [optimizers](/neural-networks/optimizers/) seen previously can each be read as
	a cheap stand-in for curvature. Momentum averages past gradients to smooth
	ill-conditioned ravines; Adam and RMSProp divide by a running estimate of each
	coordinate's gradient magnitude — a **diagonal** approximation to $H^{-1}$. They
	approximate the Newton step coordinate-by-coordinate, without ever forming the
	Hessian.
</Aside>
