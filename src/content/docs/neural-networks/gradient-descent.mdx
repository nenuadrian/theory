Backpropagation supplies the gradient $\nabla_\theta L$ — the direction of
steepest **increase** in the loss. To reduce the loss, we take a small step in the
opposite direction:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_\theta L,
$$

where $\eta$ is the **learning rate**. When $\eta$ is too small, training
progresses slowly; when it is too large, the iterate overshoots or diverges. The
update is repeated until the loss ceases to decrease.

## Descent along the surface

Consider a 2-parameter loss surface. Pressing **drop** releases a ball from the
upper ridge, which then follows $-\nabla L$ into a basin. The landscape may be
rotated by dragging; $\eta$ may then be changed and the ball released again.

This surface comprises a gentle bowl together with a ripple, the latter giving
rise to **local minima**. The two failure modes are thereby made apparent:

- **η too small** — the ball advances slowly, requires hundreds of steps, and
  settles in whichever basin lay beneath its starting position.
- **η too large** — the ball overshoots each valley and may be expelled from the
  landscape entirely (the loss *increases*).
- **η well chosen** — it descends rapidly and halts where the gradient vanishes.

<Aside type="note" title="The complete loop">
	Practical networks perform this procedure in millions of dimensions rather than
	two, with the gradient supplied by
	[backpropagation](/neural-networks/backpropagation/). The principle is
	unchanged: evaluate the loss, follow the slope downward, and repeat. This
	constitutes learning.
</Aside>
