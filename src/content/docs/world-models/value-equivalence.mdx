[PlaNet](/world-models/rssm-and-planning/) and [Dreamer](/world-models/dreamer/)
share a commitment inherited from the [VAE](/world-models/perception/): they learn
the model by **reconstructing observations**. The ELBO's first term is pixel
reconstruction, and much of the network's capacity is spent learning to render. Yet
an agent that only needs to *act* has no use for a rendered image. It needs to know
what reward an action yields and what state it leads to — quantities that may be a
vanishing fraction of what a pixel-accurate model labours to reproduce. The final
idea of the track is to discard reconstruction entirely and model only what bears on
the decision.

## The value-equivalence principle

The justification is a precise one (Grimm et al., 2020). Two models are
**value-equivalent** — with respect to a set of policies $\Pi$ and value functions
$\mathcal{V}$ — if they induce the *same Bellman updates*:

$$
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\pi} v = \mathcal{T}^{\pi} v
\qquad \text{for all } \pi \in \Pi,\; v \in \mathcal{V},
$$

where $\mathcal{T}^{\pi} v = r + \gamma P^{\pi} v$ is the [Bellman
operator](/reinforcement-learning/the-setup/). A model that is value-equivalent to
the real environment produces identical values, identical planning outcomes, and
identical policies — *even if its observations are nonsense*. Reconstruction fidelity
is simply not among the requirements. This frees the model to ignore everything in
the observation that does not affect value, and to spend its capacity where decisions
are actually made.

<Aside type="note" title="What gets thrown away">
	Consider an agent driving through rain. A reconstruction model must predict every
	raindrop, because the ELBO scores pixels; the overwhelming majority of its effort
	goes to texture that has no bearing on whether to brake. A value-equivalent model
	predicts only what changes the return — the car ahead, the road edge — and is
	indifferent to the rain. The same parameter budget buys a far better *decision*
	model.
</Aside>

## MuZero: a model trained to be searched

MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) realizes this directly. It learns three
functions, and not one of them reconstructs an observation:

$$
\underbrace{s_0 = h_\theta(o_{\le t})}_{\text{representation}}
\qquad
\underbrace{s_{k+1},\, r_k = g_\theta(s_k, a_k)}_{\text{dynamics}}
\qquad
\underbrace{p_k,\, v_k = f_\theta(s_k)}_{\text{prediction}}
$$

The representation $h$ encodes the history into an abstract root state $s_0$; the
dynamics $g$ advances the latent and predicts the reward; the prediction $f$ reads off
a policy $p_k$ and value $v_k$. The latent $s_k$ is **ungrounded** — it lives in no
observation space and is never decoded. Its only obligation is that unrolling the
model and reading off rewards, values, and policies reproduces what actually happens.
Training enforces exactly that, and nothing more: the predicted reward is matched to
the observed reward, the value to the $n$-step return, and the policy to the output of
search.

## Search as policy improvement

That last target — the policy from search — is the engine. MuZero plans with **Monte-Carlo
tree search** over its learned model. Each simulation descends the tree by the **PUCT**
rule, which balances exploitation against exploration,

$$
a = \arg\max_a \Big[\, Q(s, a) + c\, P(s, a)\, \frac{\sqrt{\sum_b N(s, b)}}{1 + N(s, a)} \,\Big],
$$

expands a leaf with the dynamics network, evaluates it with the value head, and backs
the value up the path. After a few hundred simulations, the **visit counts** at the
root form a policy markedly better than the prior $P$ the network started from — search
has done the work of [policy improvement](/reinforcement-learning/policy-gradients/).
That improved policy becomes the training target for $f$, closing the loop.

The visualization runs this search on a learned model whose prior is deliberately
*wrong*: it favours the right branch, while the best outcome lies down the left. Step
through the simulations and watch the visit counts migrate left as the value estimates
accumulate — the search overriding the prior it was given.

LEAF = {7: 0.95, 8: 0.30, 9: 0.25, 10: 0.20, 11: 0.55, 12: 0.50, 13: 0.45, 14: 0.60}

vnet = {i: float(np.clip(best_reachable(i) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(), 0, 1)) for i in range(n_total)}

        for a in (0, 1):

        N[k(node, a)] += 1; Wv[k(node, a)] += v

## JEPA: predicting in representation space

MuZero abandons reconstruction for the sake of *control*. The same move is being made
for the sake of *representation learning*, in the *Joint-Embedding Predictive
Architecture* (LeCun, 2022). A JEPA encodes a context and a target into a shared
representation space and predicts the target's **embedding** from the context's — never
the target's pixels:

$$
\min_\theta \; \big\lVert \operatorname{pred}_\theta\big(\operatorname{enc}_\theta(x_{\text{context}})\big) - \operatorname{sg}\big[\operatorname{enc}_{\bar\theta}(x_{\text{target}})\big] \big\rVert^2 .
$$

Predicting representations rather than pixels sidesteps the [multimodality
problem](/world-models/dynamics/): the encoder is free to *drop* the unpredictable
detail (the exact arrangement of leaves on a tree) and keep only what is predictable
(that there is a tree), because it is judged in its own latent space, not on
reconstruction.

<Aside type="tip" title="The collapse problem">
	Predicting one's own representation invites a trivial cheat: if the encoder maps
	*everything* to the same constant vector, the prediction is perfect and the loss is
	zero — and the representation is useless. Every non-reconstructive method must
	prevent this **collapse**. Two families of fix dominate: an **asymmetric** target
	encoder updated as a slow exponential moving average, with a **stop-gradient**
	$\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ on the target (BYOL, I-JEPA); and explicit
	**variance–covariance regularization** that forces the representation to retain
	spread and avoid redundancy (VICReg). The stop-gradient in the loss above is exactly
	this safeguard.
</Aside>

Applied to video, the architecture becomes a self-supervised world model: V-JEPA learns
the dynamics of the visual world by predicting the representations of masked future
frames, with no reconstruction and no rewards — the predictive substrate on which a
controller can later be built.

## The frontier

The two threads of this track — predict-to-*act* and predict-to-*generate* — are
converging in the present generation of large world models:

- **Genie** learns a controllable world model from unlabelled internet video,
  inferring a latent action space such that the result is *playable* frame by frame.
- **Video diffusion** models — Sora and its kin — are, in effect, implicit world
  simulators: trained only to generate, they acquire physical regularities (occlusion,
  persistence, rough dynamics) as a byproduct, and are being adapted into controllable,
  action-conditioned simulators.
- **Domain simulators** such as GAIA for driving turn the world model into a generative
  proving ground for policies that cannot be safely trained in reality.

## The thread

One idea has run through every page: **a model of the world, learned, and used to act.**
The obstacle was always the same — [error that compounds](/world-models/the-idea/) over
a rollout — and the methods are best read as three answers to it. Compress the world
into a [compact, predictable latent](/world-models/perception/), so the model is
accurate and the dynamics are smooth. [Bound the horizon](/world-models/dreamer/) and
bootstrap with a learned value, so the unbounded tail of error is never incurred. And
model [only what bears on the decision](#the-value-equivalence-principle), so capacity is
never wasted on the irrelevant. What remains open is what has always been hardest:
consistency over long horizons, abstraction and hierarchy in the latent, controllability
and causal structure, and the evaluation of models whose only true test is the
competence of the agent they produce.

<Aside type="note">
	The [Reinforcement Learning](/reinforcement-learning/) track supplied the objective
	these models serve; this track supplied the world they imagine within. Together they
	are the two halves of an agent that learns not only how to act, but what its actions
	will bring about.
</Aside>
