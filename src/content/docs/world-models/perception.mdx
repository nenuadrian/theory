The first component of a world model is **vision**: a map from a high-dimensional
observation $o$ — a frame of pixels — to a compact latent code $z$. A plain
autoencoder would suffice to compress, but it would not suffice to *predict from*
or to *sample*. Its latent space is an arbitrary, pitted surface: most points
decode to nothing meaningful, and small steps need not correspond to small changes.
A world model requires more. The dynamics model must predict the next $z$, so the
space must be smooth; the agent must be able to dream, so the space must be
samplable. The **variational autoencoder** (Kingma and Welling, 2014) is the
construction that delivers both.

## A generative model with an intractable likelihood

Posit a generative story for the data. A latent is drawn from a fixed **prior**,
and an observation is drawn from a **decoder** conditioned on it:

$$
z \sim p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad o \sim p_\theta(o \mid z).
$$

Training by maximum likelihood would maximize the **marginal likelihood**
$p_\theta(o) = \int p_\theta(o \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz$. This integral is intractable:
it marginalizes a neural-network decoder over the whole latent space. Its
inversion, the true posterior $p_\theta(z \mid o)$, is intractable for the same
reason. Both must be approximated.

## The evidence lower bound

Introduce an **encoder** $q_\phi(z \mid o)$ — an *approximate* posterior, realized
as a network that outputs the mean and variance of a Gaussian over $z$. For any
such $q_\phi$, the log-likelihood admits an exact decomposition. Multiplying inside
the logarithm by $q_\phi/q_\phi$ and separating the two ratios,

$$
\log p_\theta(o)
= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(o, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\right]}_{\textstyle \mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\,o)\;=\;\text{ELBO}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_\phi(z \mid o)\,\big\|\,p_\theta(z \mid o)\big)}_{\textstyle \ge\, 0}.
$$

The second term is the [KL divergence](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/) between
the approximate and true posteriors, and it is non-negative. The first term is
therefore a **lower bound** on the evidence — the **evidence lower bound**, or
ELBO — and because the left side does not depend on $\phi$, maximizing the ELBO in
$\phi$ simultaneously tightens the bound (drives $q_\phi$ toward the true
posterior) and, in $\theta$, raises the likelihood. Expanding
$p_\theta(o,z) = p_\theta(o \mid z)\,p(z)$ gives the form that is actually optimized:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi;\, o)
= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\!\big[\log p_\theta(o \mid z)\big]}_{\textstyle \text{reconstruction}}
\;-\;
\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_\phi(z \mid o)\,\big\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\textstyle \text{regularizer}}.
$$

Two terms, in tension. The **reconstruction** term rewards a latent that retains
enough of $o$ to rebuild it. The **regularizer** penalizes any departure of the
posterior from the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ — it is what packs the codes into a
smooth, centered, gap-free ball from which samples can later be drawn. The encoder
must compress *and* conform.

<Aside type="note" title="Why the latent ends up usable">
	The KL term is the entire reason the VAE's latent differs from an autoencoder's.
	It forbids the encoder from scattering codes across an unbounded space with empty
	regions between them. Every posterior is pulled toward the same prior, so the
	occupied region is connected and the prior's mass actually lands on plausible
	codes — which is precisely what lets the dynamics model interpolate and the agent
	sample.
</Aside>

## The same bound, from Jensen's inequality

The decomposition above is exact and names the gap explicitly, but the ELBO can be
reached by a second, more direct route worth seeing alongside it. Write the marginal
likelihood as an expectation over the encoder, by inserting $q_\phi$ into the integral:

$$
\log p_\theta(o)
= \log \int p_\theta(o, z)\, dz
= \log \int q_\phi(z \mid o)\, \frac{p_\theta(o, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\, dz
= \log \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(o, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\right].
$$

Because $\log$ is concave, **Jensen's inequality** moves it inside the expectation at
the cost of an inequality, $\log \mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X]$:

$$
\log p_\theta(o)
\;\ge\;
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(o, z)}{q_\phi(z \mid o)}\right]
= \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi;\, o).
$$

The same ELBO results, and the two derivations are complementary. The exact
decomposition quantifies the slack — it is precisely $\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z \mid o)
\,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid o)\big)$ — whereas Jensen yields the bound without measuring it.
They nonetheless agree on when it is tight: Jensen holds with equality only when the
quantity inside the expectation is constant in $z$, i.e. $p_\theta(o, z) \propto q_\phi(z
\mid o)$, which is to say $q_\phi(z \mid o) = p_\theta(z \mid o)$ — exactly the condition
under which the KL term vanishes.

## Two closed forms, and a trick

The construction is practical because both halves of the ELBO are cheap. With a
diagonal-Gaussian encoder $q_\phi(z \mid o) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\phi(o),\,
\mathrm{diag}\,\sigma_\phi^2(o)\big)$ and the standard normal prior, the KL has a
**closed form**:

$$
\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \mathrm{diag}\,\sigma^2)\,\big\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big)
= \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big(\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_i^2\big).
$$

The reconstruction term is an expectation over $q_\phi$, estimated by sampling a
$z$. But sampling blocks the gradient: $\nabla_\phi$ cannot pass through a draw from
a distribution whose parameters are themselves being differentiated. The
**reparameterization trick** removes the obstruction by pushing the randomness into
a parameter-free variable,

$$
z = \mu_\phi(o) + \sigma_\phi(o) \odot \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
$$

so that $z$ is now a *deterministic, differentiable* function of $\phi$ and an
external noise $\varepsilon$. The gradient flows through $\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi$
exactly as [backpropagation](/neural-networks/backpropagation/) requires, and the
whole ELBO is optimized by ordinary gradient ascent.

<Aside type="tip" title="β and the rate–distortion trade">
	Weighting the regularizer by a coefficient $\beta$ — maximizing
	$\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(o \mid z)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}$ — turns the ELBO into a
	dial between two regimes. Large $\beta$ buys a tidier, more disentangled, more
	samplable latent at the cost of blurrier reconstructions (the **β-VAE**); small
	$\beta$ recovers a near-autoencoder. The dial is exactly the **rate–distortion**
	trade: the KL is the *rate* (bits spent on the code), the reconstruction is the
	*distortion*.
</Aside>

## A VAE, from scratch

The cell below trains a complete variational autoencoder in NumPy — encoder,
reparameterized sampling, decoder, the closed-form KL, and manual backpropagation
through all of it. The data lie on a noisy ring: a one-dimensional manifold (the
angle) embedded in two dimensions, which a **one-dimensional** latent is exactly
able to capture. After training, sampling $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and decoding
produces new points that land back on the ring — the model has learned not merely
to compress the data but to *generate* it.

<PyCell
	client:only="react"
	id="wm-vae-ring"
	title="vae.py"
	code={`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
np.seterr(divide="ignore", over="ignore", invalid="ignore")  # clean output everywhere

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Data: a noisy ring. A 1-D manifold (the angle) sitting in 2-D.
N = 600
ang = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, N)
X = np.stack([np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang)], 1) + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal((N, 2))

# A VAE with a 1-D latent. Encoder and decoder are one-hidden-layer tanh MLPs.
H, D = 24, 1
def init(a, b): return rng.standard_normal((a, b)) * np.sqrt(2.0 / a)
P = dict(We1=init(2, H), be1=np.zeros(H), Wmu=init(H, D) * 0.1, bmu=np.zeros(D),
         Wlv=init(H, D) * 0.1, blv=np.zeros(D), Wd1=init(D, H), bd1=np.zeros(H),
         Wd2=init(H, 2), bd2=np.zeros(2))
m = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
v = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}

beta_max, warmup, lr, B = 0.2, 800, 2e-3, 128   # beta-VAE weight + KL warmup
rec_hist, kl_hist = [], []
for it in range(4000):
    beta = beta_max * min(1.0, it / warmup)
    x = X[rng.integers(0, N, B)]
    # --- encode ---
    he = np.tanh(x @ P["We1"] + P["be1"])
    mu = he @ P["Wmu"] + P["bmu"]
    logvar = np.clip(he @ P["Wlv"] + P["blv"], -8, 8)
    std = np.exp(0.5 * logvar)
    eps = rng.standard_normal((B, D))
    z = mu + std * eps                              # reparameterization trick
    # --- decode ---
    hd = np.tanh(z @ P["Wd1"] + P["bd1"])
    xh = hd @ P["Wd2"] + P["bd2"]
    # --- ELBO = -recon - beta * KL ---
    recon = np.mean(np.sum((xh - x) ** 2, 1))       # Gaussian decoder
    kl = np.mean(0.5 * np.sum(mu ** 2 + np.exp(logvar) - 1 - logvar, 1))
    rec_hist.append(recon); kl_hist.append(kl)
    # --- backprop ---
    dxh = 2 * (xh - x) / B
    g = {}
    g["Wd2"] = hd.T @ dxh; g["bd2"] = dxh.sum(0)
    dzd = (dxh @ P["Wd2"].T) * (1 - hd ** 2)
    g["Wd1"] = z.T @ dzd; g["bd1"] = dzd.sum(0)
    dz = dzd @ P["Wd1"].T
    dmu = dz + beta * mu / B                          # recon grad + KL grad
    dlogvar = dz * (eps * 0.5 * std) + beta * 0.5 * (np.exp(logvar) - 1) / B
    g["Wmu"] = he.T @ dmu; g["bmu"] = dmu.sum(0)
    g["Wlv"] = he.T @ dlogvar; g["blv"] = dlogvar.sum(0)
    dze = (dmu @ P["Wmu"].T + dlogvar @ P["Wlv"].T) * (1 - he ** 2)
    g["We1"] = x.T @ dze; g["be1"] = dze.sum(0)
    gn = np.sqrt(sum((gg ** 2).sum() for gg in g.values()))
    scale = min(1.0, 5.0 / (gn + 1e-8))             # global gradient clip
    for k in P:                                      # Adam
        gk = g[k] * scale
        m[k] = 0.9 * m[k] + 0.1 * gk
        v[k] = 0.999 * v[k] + 0.001 * gk ** 2
        mh = m[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** (it + 1)); vh = v[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** (it + 1))
        P[k] -= lr * mh / (np.sqrt(vh) + 1e-8)

def encode(x): return np.tanh(x @ P["We1"] + P["be1"]) @ P["Wmu"] + P["bmu"]
def decode(z): return np.tanh(z @ P["Wd1"] + P["bd1"]) @ P["Wd2"] + P["bd2"]

print(f"reconstruction MSE: {rec_hist[0]:.3f} -> {np.mean(rec_hist[-50:]):.3f}")
print(f"KL to prior:        {kl_hist[0]:.3f} -> {np.mean(kl_hist[-50:]):.3f}  (latent in use)")
gen = decode(rng.standard_normal((400, 1)))
print(f"prior samples decode to radius {np.sqrt((gen ** 2).sum(1)).mean():.3f}  (data ring = 1.0)")

recon_X = decode(encode(X))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(7.2, 2.6))
ax[0].scatter(X[:, 0], X[:, 1], s=6, c="#888888"); ax[0].set_title("data", fontsize=9)
ax[1].scatter(recon_X[:, 0], recon_X[:, 1], s=6, c="#2dd4bf"); ax[1].set_title("reconstruction", fontsize=9)
ax[2].scatter(gen[:, 0], gen[:, 1], s=6, c="#fb923c"); ax[2].set_title("decode(z ~ N(0,1))", fontsize=9)
for a in ax:
    a.set_aspect("equal"); a.set_xticks([]); a.set_yticks([]); a.set_xlim(-1.6, 1.6); a.set_ylim(-1.6, 1.6)
fig.tight_layout()
`}
/>

The KL climbing away from zero is the signal that the latent is being *used*: had
$\beta$ been too large, the encoder would have collapsed to the prior, the KL would
have stayed at zero, and the decoder would have produced the data mean — the
**posterior collapse** that the warm-up and the modest $\beta$ are chosen to avoid.

The VAE supplies the latent $z_t$. What it does not supply is any sense of *time*:
it sees each observation in isolation and has no notion of what comes next.
Endowing the latent with dynamics — a model of $p(z_{t+1} \mid z_t, a_t)$ — is the
[next component](/world-models/dynamics/).
