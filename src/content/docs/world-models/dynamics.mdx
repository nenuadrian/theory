The [variational autoencoder](/world-models/perception/) gives the agent a latent
$z_t$, but no sense of time. The second component supplies it: a **dynamics model**
that predicts the next latent from the current latent and the action taken. Because
a single latent is not a [sufficient state](/world-models/the-idea/) under partial
observability, the model carries a recurrent summary $h_t$ of the entire past,

$$
h_t = f_\theta\big(h_{t-1},\, z_{t-1},\, a_{t-1}\big),
$$

realized by an LSTM or GRU. The pair $(z_t, h_t)$ is the state the agent actually
acts on. The model's task is the conditional distribution of the next latent,
$p_\theta(z_{t+1} \mid z_t, a_t, h_t)$ — and the form of that distribution is the
crux of the matter.

## The future is not a point

It is tempting to predict $z_{t+1}$ with a single vector and train it by mean
squared error. This fails, and the reason is fundamental rather than technical. The
future is **uncertain**: a corridor may branch left or right, a die may land on any
face, an object may topple either way. When several outcomes are each valid, the
MSE-optimal prediction is their **conditional mean** — and the mean of two valid
futures is, in general, *not a valid future*. Averaging a left turn and a right turn
yields driving straight into the wall.

A correct dynamics model must therefore represent a genuinely **multimodal**
distribution over next latents, placing mass on each possible continuation rather
than smearing it across the invalid average. The visualization below makes the
failure literal: imagined latent rollouts bifurcate into two modes, and the
deterministic mean predictor (amber) runs straight down the empty gap between them.

## The mixture-density network

The instrument for predicting a multimodal target is the **mixture-density network**
(Bishop, 1994): rather than emit a point, the network emits the parameters of a
mixture of Gaussians. From the recurrent state $h_t$, a head produces $K$ mixing
weights, means, and scales,

$$
p_\theta(z_{t+1} \mid z_t, a_t, h_t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k(h_t)\,
\mathcal{N}\!\big(z_{t+1};\, \mu_k(h_t),\, \sigma_k^2(h_t)\,I\big),
\qquad
\sum_k \pi_k = 1,
$$

with the weights $\pi_k$ produced by a softmax and the scales $\sigma_k$ by an
exponential, so both obey their constraints. The composite — a recurrent network
feeding a mixture-density head — is the **MDN-RNN**, the memory module $M$ of the
original World Models. It is trained not by MSE but by the **negative
log-likelihood** of the realized next latent,

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_t \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k(h_t)\,
\mathcal{N}\!\big(z_{t+1};\, \mu_k(h_t),\, \sigma_k^2(h_t)\big),
$$

which rewards placing a mode wherever an outcome actually occurs and is indifferent
to the gaps between modes.

<Aside type="note" title="Responsibilities">
	The NLL gradient is governed by the posterior **responsibility** of each
	component, $\gamma_k = \pi_k \mathcal{N}_k / \sum_j \pi_j \mathcal{N}_j$ — the
	probability that component $k$ generated the observed target. The mean of
	component $k$ is pulled toward the target in proportion to $\gamma_k$, so each
	mode is drawn only toward the outcomes it is already responsible for, and the
	components specialize onto distinct branches rather than all collapsing to the
	mean. This is an EM-like division of labour, learned by gradient descent.
</Aside>

## Dreaming, with temperature

Once trained, the MDN-RNN *is* the world: feeding a sampled $z_{t+1}$ back in as the
next input generates a trajectory entirely in imagination — a **dream**. The fidelity
of the dream is governed by a single scalar, the **temperature** $\tau$, which
rescales the model's uncertainty at sampling time:

$$
\sigma_k \;\longmapsto\; \tau\,\sigma_k, \qquad
\pi_k \;\propto\; \pi_k^{1/\tau}.
$$

At $\tau \to 0$ the dream is nearly deterministic, following the single most likely
continuation; at $\tau = 1$ it samples faithfully from the learned distribution; at
$\tau > 1$ it grows more varied and chaotic. Ha and Schmidhuber observed something
counterintuitive: training the controller in a dream made *more* uncertain than
reality — a higher $\tau$ — yields policies that transfer **better** to the real
environment. A harder, noisier dream is a regularizer; a policy that survives it is
robust to the places where the model is wrong, and so does not exploit the model's
fantasies.

## A mixture-density network, from scratch

The cell below fits an MDN to the canonical one-to-many problem. The forward map
$x = t + 0.3\sin(2\pi t)$ is non-monotone, so its inverse has up to **three** valid
branches over the central range — exactly the multimodal target that defeats MSE. The
MDN learns all three branches; an MSE network of identical size, trained alongside,
learns the single curve threading their average, which passes through regions the
data never occupies.

	code={`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
np.seterr(divide="ignore", over="ignore", invalid="ignore")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# A one-to-many target: x = t + 0.3 sin(2 pi t) is non-monotone, so the inverse
# t(x) has up to three valid branches. MSE cannot represent that; a mixture can.
n = 1000
t = rng.uniform(0, 1, (n, 1))
x = t + 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t) + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal((n, 1))

Hd, K = 24, 3                                    # hidden width, mixture components
def init(a, b): return rng.standard_normal((a, b)) * np.sqrt(2.0 / a)
def softmax(a):
    a = a - a.max(1, keepdims=True); e = np.exp(a); return e / e.sum(1, keepdims=True)

P = dict(W1=init(1, Hd), b1=np.zeros(Hd), Wa=init(Hd, K) * 0.1, ba=np.zeros(K),
         Wm=init(Hd, K), bm=np.zeros(K), Ws=init(Hd, K) * 0.1, bs=np.zeros(K))
m = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
v = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}

for it in range(4000):
    h = np.tanh(x @ P["W1"] + P["b1"])
    pi = softmax(h @ P["Wa"] + P["ba"])          # mixture weights
    mu = h @ P["Wm"] + P["bm"]                    # component means
    sig = np.exp(np.clip(h @ P["Ws"] + P["bs"], -6, 4))   # component scales
    Nk = np.exp(-0.5 * ((t - mu) / sig) ** 2) / (sig * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
    p = (pi * Nk).sum(1, keepdims=True) + 1e-12
    nll = -np.mean(np.log(p))
    gamma = pi * Nk / p                           # responsibilities
    da = (pi - gamma) / n                         # MDN output gradients (Bishop)
    dmu = gamma * (mu - t) / sig ** 2 / n
    ds = gamma * (1 - ((t - mu) / sig) ** 2) / n
    g = {"Wa": h.T @ da, "ba": da.sum(0), "Wm": h.T @ dmu, "bm": dmu.sum(0),
         "Ws": h.T @ ds, "bs": ds.sum(0)}
    dh = (da @ P["Wa"].T + dmu @ P["Wm"].T + ds @ P["Ws"].T) * (1 - h ** 2)
    g["W1"] = x.T @ dh; g["b1"] = dh.sum(0)
    for k in P:
        m[k] = 0.9 * m[k] + 0.1 * g[k]; v[k] = 0.999 * v[k] + 0.001 * g[k] ** 2
        P[k] -= 3e-3 * (m[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** (it + 1))) / (np.sqrt(v[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** (it + 1))) + 1e-8)

# An MSE network of identical size, for comparison.
Q = dict(W1=init(1, Hd), b1=np.zeros(Hd), W2=init(Hd, 1), b2=np.zeros(1))
mq = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in Q.items()}
vq = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in Q.items()}
for it in range(4000):
    h = np.tanh(x @ Q["W1"] + Q["b1"]); yh = h @ Q["W2"] + Q["b2"]
    dyh = 2 * (yh - t) / n
    g = {"W2": h.T @ dyh, "b2": dyh.sum(0)}
    dh = (dyh @ Q["W2"].T) * (1 - h ** 2)
    g["W1"] = x.T @ dh; g["b1"] = dh.sum(0)
    for k in Q:
        mq[k] = 0.9 * mq[k] + 0.1 * g[k]; vq[k] = 0.999 * vq[k] + 0.001 * g[k] ** 2
        Q[k] -= 3e-3 * (mq[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** (it + 1))) / (np.sqrt(vq[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** (it + 1))) + 1e-8)

print(f"MDN negative log-likelihood: {nll:.3f}")
hc = np.tanh(np.array([[0.5]]) @ P["W1"] + P["b1"])
print("at x=0.5  mixture means", np.round((hc @ P["Wm"] + P["bm"])[0], 2),
      " weights", np.round(softmax(hc @ P["Wa"] + P["ba"])[0], 2), " (three branches)")

# Sample t ~ p(t|x) from the trained mixture across the input range.
xs = np.linspace(x.min(), x.max(), 1200).reshape(-1, 1)
h = np.tanh(xs @ P["W1"] + P["b1"])
pis = softmax(h @ P["Wa"] + P["ba"]); mus = h @ P["Wm"] + P["bm"]
sigs = np.exp(np.clip(h @ P["Ws"] + P["bs"], -6, 4))
comp = (rng.random((len(xs), 1)) < np.cumsum(pis, 1)).argmax(1)
idx = np.arange(len(xs))
ts = mus[idx, comp] + sigs[idx, comp] * rng.standard_normal(len(xs))
mse_pred = np.tanh(xs @ Q["W1"] + Q["b1"]) @ Q["W2"] + Q["b2"]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.4))
ax.scatter(x, t, s=5, c="#888888", alpha=0.4, label="data (one-to-many)")
ax.scatter(xs, ts, s=5, c="#2dd4bf", alpha=0.5, label="MDN samples")
ax.plot(xs, mse_pred, c="#fb923c", lw=2.5, label="MSE net = conditional mean")
ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("t"); ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc="upper left")
ax.set_title("the mixture finds the branches; the mean falls between them")
fig.tight_layout()
`}

This pairing — a VAE for perception and an MDN-RNN for dynamics — is the complete
World Models architecture, and it is enough to train a controller in a dream. It has
one structural limitation: the VAE is trained *first and separately*, optimizing for
reconstruction alone, with no pressure to produce a latent that is easy to *predict*.
The next page removes that separation. PlaNet's **recurrent state-space model** learns
representation and dynamics together, in a single latent that is at once deterministic
and stochastic, and plans within it.
