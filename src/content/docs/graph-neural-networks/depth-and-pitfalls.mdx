We now have a family of architectures — GCN, GAT, and every other message-passing
scheme. Two questions determine how far it can carry us. **How expressive is it?**
And **what happens when it is made deep?** The answers are precise, and somewhat
sobering.

## How powerful is message passing?

There is a classic algorithm for distinguishing graphs: the **Weisfeiler–Leman**
(1-WL) colour-refinement test. Every node is assigned a colour, and each node is
then repeatedly recoloured by hashing its own colour together with the
**multiset** of its neighbours' colours:

$$
c_i^{(k)} = \operatorname{HASH}\Big( c_i^{(k-1)},\; \{\!\!\{\, c_j^{(k-1)} : j \in \mathcal{N}(i) \,\}\!\!\} \Big).
$$

If two graphs end with different colour histograms, they are not isomorphic. The
correspondence is evident — this is *precisely* message passing, with a hash in
place of the neural aggregator. That parallel is a theorem: **a message-passing GNN
is at most as powerful as the 1-WL test** at distinguishing graphs. No
parametrization escapes that ceiling.

To *reach* the ceiling, the aggregate-and-update step must be **injective** on
multisets — distinct neighbourhoods must always produce distinct outputs. Here the
choice of aggregator becomes decisive:

- **mean** loses counts: $\{\!\!\{a\}\!\!\}$ and $\{\!\!\{a,a\}\!\!\}$ average to the same thing.
- **max** loses multiplicity: $\{\!\!\{a,b\}\!\!\}$ and $\{\!\!\{a,b,b\}\!\!\}$ have the same max.
- **sum** retains both, as it can count.

This is the insight behind the **Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN)**, which uses a sum
together with an MLP to obtain an injective update, making it provably as powerful
as 1-WL:

$$
\mathbf{h}_i^{(k)} = \operatorname{MLP}^{(k)}\!\Big( (1+\epsilon)\,\mathbf{h}_i^{(k-1)} + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{h}_j^{(k-1)} \Big).
$$

<Aside type="caution" title="The ceiling is genuine">
	1-WL — and therefore every standard GNN — **cannot** distinguish certain
	non-isomorphic graphs: it cannot reliably count triangles or separate certain
	regular graphs. Two structurally distinct molecules may be indistinguishable to
	it. This motivates higher-order GNNs, positional and structural encodings, and
	subgraph methods that deliberately exceed 1-WL.
</Aside>

## Failure mode #1: over-smoothing

Expressiveness is thus bounded — but does additional depth at least help within
that bound? Frequently it does not. Each layer mixes neighbours, and mixing is
*smoothing*. Applying the propagation operator $\hat{A}$ a sufficient number of
times causes every node to converge toward the same vector (formally, the
projection onto $\hat{A}$'s dominant eigenvector). The **Dirichlet energy** — the
total disagreement across edges — decays to zero:

$$
E(H) = \sum_{(i,j)\in E} \Big\| \frac{\mathbf{h}_i}{\sqrt{\tilde d_i}} - \frac{\mathbf{h}_j}{\sqrt{\tilde d_j}} \Big\|^2 \;\xrightarrow[\text{layers}]{}\; 0.
$$

In the visualization below, the two communities — sharply separated at layer 0 —
dissolve into a single indistinguishable cluster as the slider advances, and the
energy curve declines steeply.

This is why vanilla GCNs are typically **shallow**, with two or three layers. Beyond
that, the features the classifier requires have been averaged away. The same
collapse, measured numerically, is shown below:

## Failure mode #2: over-squashing

The opposite problem afflicts long-range tasks. A node's receptive field grows
*exponentially* with depth, while its representation remains a *fixed-size* vector.
Information from distant nodes must therefore be compressed through that bottleneck,
and through the few edges that bridge distant regions. Signals required to travel
far are **over-squashed**, and the network cannot route them. Adding layers to reach
the distant node only intensifies the squashing.

## What actually helps

- **Remain shallow and increase width.** Most graph tasks are local, and 2–4 layers
  is typical.
- **Residual or skip connections and jumping knowledge.** These allow later layers
  to retain access to earlier, un-smoothed representations.
- **Normalization** (PairNorm, GraphNorm), which explicitly prevents node features
  from collapsing together.
- **Attention and gating** ([GAT](/graph-neural-networks/graph-attention/)), which
  down-weight uninformative neighbours rather than averaging indiscriminately.
- **Graph rewiring**, which widens bottlenecks and relieves over-squashing for
  long-range tasks.
- **Global attention** ([graph transformers](/graph-neural-networks/graph-transformers/)),
  which abandon locality entirely — every node attends to every node, so a
  long-range signal need not be routed through any bottleneck at all.

<Aside type="note" title="The whole track, summarized">
	A graph carries data with no canonical order, which calls for a
	permutation-respecting computation — [message passing](/graph-neural-networks/graphs-and-message-passing/).
	Taking its aggregator to be a principled normalized average yields the
	[GCN](/graph-neural-networks/graph-convolutional-networks/); making the weights
	learned and content-dependent yields
	[GAT](/graph-neural-networks/graph-attention/). The family is bounded by the
	power of the 1-WL test, and depth is opposed by over-smoothing and
	over-squashing. The objective, within this local family, is to remain shallow,
	select the appropriate aggregator, and facilitate the flow of signals. Where the
	required range exceeds what locality can carry, the
	[next page](/graph-neural-networks/graph-transformers/) discards the edge mask
	altogether for global attention.
</Aside>
