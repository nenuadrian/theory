The second road through reinforcement learning is an instance of a procedure that
predates it by decades: **expectation maximization** (EM). Before specializing it
to control, it is worth stating EM in its canonical form — as the standard remedy
for maximum-likelihood estimation when each observation is accompanied by an
unobserved, or *latent*, variable. The structure established here — a lower bound
on the objective, an alternation between completing the data and refitting the
model, and a guarantee that the likelihood cannot decrease — transfers intact to
the policy-optimization setting of the pages that follow.

## The obstacle: a latent variable

Consider a model $p(x, z; \theta)$ over an observed variable $x$ and a latent
variable $z$, with parameters $\theta$. Only $x$ is seen; $z$ must be summed out
to obtain the quantity maximum likelihood maximizes, the **log-evidence**

$$
\log p(x; \theta) \;=\; \log \sum_{z} p(x, z; \theta).
$$

The logarithm of a sum does not separate, so neither a closed form nor a clean
gradient is generally available. The underlying difficulty is circular: the best
parameters depend on which latent value generated each point, and that assignment
depends in turn on the parameters.

The canonical instance is a **mixture of Gaussians**. Each point $x_i$ is drawn
from one of $K$ components, but the component identity $z_i$ is unobserved. Were
the labels known, fitting each component would be elementary; were the parameters
known, the labels would follow from Bayes' rule. EM resolves the circularity by
alternating between the two.

## A lower bound on the evidence

Introduce *any* distribution $q(z)$ over the latent. For every such $q$, the
log-evidence admits an exact decomposition:

$$
\log p(x; \theta) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{q(z)}\right]}_{\textstyle \mathcal{L}(q, \theta)} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z \mid x; \theta)\big)}_{\textstyle \ge\, 0}.
$$

The identity follows from $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\,p(x)$ and the fact that
$\log p(x;\theta)$ does not depend on $z$. The first term $\mathcal{L}(q, \theta)$
is the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO); the second is the KL divergence between
$q$ and the true posterior, which is nonnegative. Hence

$$
\mathcal{L}(q, \theta) \;\le\; \log p(x; \theta), \qquad \text{with equality if and only if } q(z) = p(z \mid x; \theta).
$$

Two features of this decomposition drive everything that follows. It is an
**exact identity**, not an approximation. And because its left-hand side is
independent of $q$, any gain in $\mathcal{L}$ at fixed $\theta$ is offset by an
equal reduction in the KL gap — raising the bound tightens it.

<Aside type="note" title="The direction of the gap">
	The gap is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(z \mid x))$, with $q$ in the first
	argument — the [**reverse** KL](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/) that
	variational methods always minimize. EM is the special case in which $q$ is
	unconstrained, so the gap can be driven to *exactly* zero rather than merely
	made small.
</Aside>

## The two steps

EM is coordinate ascent on the single function $\mathcal{L}(q, \theta)$:
maximize it over $q$, then over $\theta$, and repeat.

**E-step (expectation).** Hold $\theta = \theta^{(t)}$ fixed and maximize
$\mathcal{L}$ over $q$. The only $q$-dependent term is the KL gap, so the
maximizer is the value that closes it:

$$
q^{(t)}(z) \;=\; p\big(z \mid x; \theta^{(t)}\big).
$$

The latent is *completed* by its posterior under the current parameters, and the
bound now touches the log-evidence at $\theta^{(t)}$.

**M-step (maximization).** Hold $q = q^{(t)}$ fixed and maximize $\mathcal{L}$
over $\theta$. Discarding the $\theta$-independent entropy $-\mathbb{E}_q[\log q]$,
this is

$$
\theta^{(t+1)} \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{q^{(t)}}\!\big[\log p(x, z; \theta)\big].
$$

The objective is the **expected complete-data log-likelihood** — the likelihood
one could write if the latent were observed, averaged over the posterior just
computed. With the assignment filled in, the maximization is the tractable,
fully-observed one.

## Why it cannot decrease the likelihood

The two steps chain into a monotonicity guarantee. After the E-step the bound is
tight, $\mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) = \log p(x; \theta^{(t)})$. The M-step
raises the bound, $\mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)})$.
And the bound lies below the evidence everywhere. Concatenating,

$$
\log p(x; \theta^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) \;=\; \log p(x; \theta^{(t)}).
$$

The likelihood is **nondecreasing at every iteration**. EM never takes a backward
step — a guarantee no plain gradient ascent on $\log p$ provides without a
line search. It converges to a stationary point of the likelihood, though not
necessarily the global maximum; the bound is a *minorizer* that touches the
objective at the current iterate, which makes EM the prototypical
minorize-maximize scheme.

## EM on a Gaussian mixture

The mixture model makes each step concrete. The E-step assigns each point a
**responsibility** — the posterior probability that it belongs to each component.
The M-step refits each component's mean and variance as a responsibility-weighted
average, and the mixing weights as the average responsibility. The cell fits two
one-dimensional Gaussians to unlabelled data and records the log-likelihood at
every iteration.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Ground-truth two-component mixture. EM observes x only, never the labels z.
n = 400
z = rng.random(n) < 0.35
x = np.where(z, rng.normal(6.0, 1.0, n), rng.normal(1.0, 1.5, n))

def normal_pdf(x, mu, var):
    return np.exp(-(x - mu) ** 2 / (2 * var)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)

# Initialize deliberately away from the truth.
mu  = np.array([0.0, 3.0])
var = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
pi  = np.array([0.5, 0.5])

loglik = []
for step in range(30):
    # E-step: responsibilities r[k, i] = p(z = k | x_i) under current params.
    comp  = np.stack([pi[k] * normal_pdf(x, mu[k], var[k]) for k in range(2)])
    total = comp.sum(0)
    r     = comp / total
    loglik.append(np.log(total).sum())
    # M-step: maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood (closed form).
    Nk  = r.sum(1)
    pi  = Nk / n
    mu  = (r * x).sum(1) / Nk
    var = (r * (x - mu[:, None]) ** 2).sum(1) / Nk

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 3.2))
grid = np.linspace(x.min() - 1, x.max() + 1, 300)
axL.hist(x, bins=40, density=True, color="lightgray")
for k in range(2):
    axL.plot(grid, pi[k] * normal_pdf(grid, mu[k], var[k]), lw=2)
axL.set_title("data and the two fitted components")
axR.plot(loglik, marker="o", ms=3, color="teal")
axR.set_title("data log-likelihood per EM step")
axR.set_xlabel("iteration")
fig.tight_layout()

order = np.argsort(mu)
print("recovered means:    ", np.round(mu[order], 2), "  (truth 1.0, 6.0)")
print("recovered weights:  ", np.round(pi[order], 2))
print("monotone increasing:", bool(np.all(np.diff(loglik) >= -1e-9)))
`}

<Aside type="tip" title="What the two steps are doing">
	The responsibilities are the E-step's posterior — soft, fractional labels
	rather than hard assignments. The weighted moments are the M-step, the
	closed-form maximizer of the expected complete-data likelihood. The
	right-hand curve is the monotonicity guarantee made visible: it rises at every
	step and never falls.

## The same skeleton, applied to control

The next page retains every component of this procedure and changes only its
interpretation. The correspondence is exact:

| General EM | Control as inference |
| --- | --- |
| observed variable $x$ | the event that behaviour was optimal, $\mathcal{O} = 1$ |
| latent variable $z$ | the action $a$ |
| posterior $p(z \mid x)$ | the optimal policy $\pi^{*} \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(A/\alpha)$ |
| expected complete-data log-likelihood | advantage-weighted log-likelihood |
| monotone $\log p(x)$ | monotone policy improvement |

The E-step computes a posterior over actions — a reweighted policy $\pi^{*}$ — and
the M-step fits the network to it. One distinction is worth flagging in advance.
In the mixture model the M-step maximizes the expected complete-data likelihood
directly; in control the target $\pi^{*}$ is *nonparametric* and must be
**projected** onto the network by minimizing the forward KL
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$ — the
[direction maximum likelihood minimizes](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/). That
projection is itself a weighted maximum likelihood, so the two coincide in form.

Continue to [RL as Inference](/reinforcement-learning/rl-as-inference/), where the
optimality variable is defined and the E-step posterior is derived in closed form.
