Supervised learning provides a teacher: every input arrives paired with the
correct answer. Reinforcement learning provides only a **critic** that scores an
action after it has been taken. An agent acts, the environment responds with a
reward, and the sole objective is to **act so as to accumulate more reward over
time**. The correct action is never disclosed.

This single distinction — feedback that is *evaluative* (how good was that?)
rather than *instructive* (here is the answer) — is what makes RL difficult, and
it accounts for the proliferation of algorithms that appear superficially
distinct. This track demonstrates that most modern methods are two answers to
the same question.

## One objective, two roads

Every method considered here optimizes the **same objective** — maximization of
the expected return
$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t\right]$ over the
parameters $\theta$ of a policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$. The algorithms differ in
*how* they improve $\theta$:

- **Differentiate and ascend.** Estimate $\nabla_\theta J$ from sampled
  experience and step uphill, taking care that a single update cannot collapse
  the policy. This is the **policy-gradient** route: `REINFORCE → actor-critic →
  TRPO → PPO → GRPO`.
- **Lower-bound and alternate.** Here the policy is not differentiated at all.
  One first determines, in closed form, the form of a *better* policy (an
  **E-step**), then trains the network to imitate it by weighted regression (an
  **M-step**), and repeats. This is the **expectation-maximization** route — the
  classical EM algorithm, specialized to control:
  `EM → RL-as-inference → MPO → V-MPO → AWR`.

The central result of the track is that **the two routes converge**. Both keep
the updated policy close to a reference policy in **KL divergence**. PPO and GRPO
impose that KL as a penalty because it is effective in practice; the inference
view *derives* it as the exact, optimal choice. Recognizing this reduces the
apparent diversity of methods to a single picture.
