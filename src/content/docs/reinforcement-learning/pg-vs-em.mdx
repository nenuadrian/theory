The two roads of this track were developed separately and read differently, yet
they optimize a single objective and target a single optimum. This page sets them
side by side: what they share, where their mechanisms diverge, and which to prefer
for a given problem.

## The shared objective

Every method considered here — REINFORCE, [PPO](/reinforcement-learning/trust-regions/),
and [GRPO](/reinforcement-learning/grpo/) on one side;
[MPO](/reinforcement-learning/mpo/), [V-MPO](/reinforcement-learning/vmpo/), and
[AWR](/reinforcement-learning/awr/) on the other — improves the same
**KL-regularized return**

$$
J(\pi) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\big[A(s, a)\big] \;-\; \alpha\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid s)\big).
$$

Collect advantage, but do not depart too far from a reference policy. A single
objective has a single maximizer — the
[closed-form optimal policy](/reinforcement-learning/rl-as-inference/)

$$
\pi^{*}(a \mid s) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid s)\, \exp\!\big(A(s, a) / \alpha\big).
$$

Both roads are therefore procedures for moving a parametric policy $\pi_\theta$
toward this same $\pi^{*}$. They differ only in *how* they move it.

## Two procedures, one summit

**Policy gradient — ascend.** The policy gradient estimates $\nabla_\theta J$ from
sampled actions and takes a bounded step uphill. The policy is differentiated
through $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$; the KL is held in check by a clip
(PPO, GRPO) or a hard constraint (TRPO). Each update is a small, first-order move,
and many are required to arrive.

**Expectation maximization — jump and imitate.** The inference road never
differentiates the policy. It evaluates $\pi^{*}$ in closed form (the E-step) and
fits $\pi_\theta$ to it by advantage-weighted regression (the M-step). The
improvement per iteration is the full jump to the optimum of the current bound,
followed by a supervised projection onto the network.

<Aside type="note" title="Same hill, two ascents">
	Both procedures address the same KL-regularized hill, whose summit is
	$\pi^{*}$. One side differentiates its way up, step by bounded step; the other
	reads off the summit in closed form and regresses onto it. The objective and
	the optimum are shared; only the route differs.
</Aside>

## Side by side

| | Policy gradient (PG) | Expectation maximization (EM) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Members | REINFORCE, A2C, TRPO, PPO, GRPO | MPO, V-MPO, AWR, AWAC |
| Policy update | gradient ascent on $J$ | weighted regression onto $\pi^{*}$ |
| Differentiates the policy? | yes, through $\nabla_\theta \log \pi$ | no; the policy is only a regression target |
| Improvement per step | small, first-order | full jump to the bound's optimum |
| Advantage signal | on-policy $A$, often via GAE | learned $Q$ (MPO) or $A$ (V-MPO, AWR) |
| Trust region | explicit: clip or KL constraint | the E-step KL penalty, or implicit in regressing on buffered data |
| Data regime | typically on-policy, fresh rollouts | naturally off-policy: replay or offline |
| Sample reuse | limited — data goes stale | high — regress repeatedly on a buffer |
| Principal failure mode | high-variance gradient, destructive steps | inaccurate $Q$ or posterior, over-concentrated weights |
| Language-model instance | GRPO | DPO, the closed-form limit |

## Where the seam is

The two roads meet at [AWR and AWAC](/reinforcement-learning/awr/). Expand the
exponentiated-advantage weight that defines the M-step,
$\exp(A/\alpha) = 1 + A/\alpha + \tfrac{1}{2}(A/\alpha)^2 + \cdots$, and consider
the regression gradient evaluated at $\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}$:

$$
\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\big[\exp(A/\alpha)\, \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big] \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big]}_{=\,0} \;+\; \frac{1}{\alpha}\, \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[A\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big]}_{\text{the policy gradient}} \;+\; \cdots
$$

The leading term vanishes because the score has zero mean, and the next term is
exactly the policy gradient, scaled by $1/\alpha$. A single EM step in the
**high-temperature limit is a policy-gradient step**. The genuine difference is
finite temperature: EM retains the entire exponential weight — a finite jump
toward $\pi^{*}$ — whereas PG retains only its linearization, an infinitesimal
step in the same direction.

## Muesli: one loss, both roads

AWR is a seam of *coincidence* — the two roads turn out to be the same update in a
limit. [Muesli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06159) (Hessel et al., 2021) is a seam of
*construction*: rather than letting one road collapse into the other, it takes a term
from each and descends their sum. Its policy loss is, in the paper's notation,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}+\text{CMPO}}(\pi, s) \;=\; \underbrace{-\,\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\big[\hat A(s, a)\big]}_{\text{policy gradient — ascend}} \;+\; \lambda\, \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\text{CMPO}}(\cdot \mid s) \,\big\|\, \pi(\cdot \mid s)\big)}_{\text{EM — regress onto the optimum}}.
$$

The first term is the policy-gradient road in objective form: its gradient is the
score-function estimate of $\nabla_\theta J$, here fed by multi-step (Retrace)
advantages so the signal carries little bias. The second term is the EM road, written
as a regression onto a target

$$
\pi_{\text{CMPO}}(a \mid s) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{prior}}(a \mid s)\, \exp\!\big(\mathrm{clip}(\hat A(s, a),\, -c,\, c)\big),
$$

where $\pi_{\text{prior}}$ is Muesli's name for the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
This is exactly the [closed-form optimum](/reinforcement-learning/rl-as-inference/)
$\pi^{*}$ of the shared objective — reference policy times exponentiated advantage —
with the advantage normalized and clipped to $[-c, c]$. Minimizing the forward KL
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{CMPO}} \,\|\, \pi)$ is a weighted maximum-likelihood
projection onto that target: the [MPO](/reinforcement-learning/mpo/) M-step in all but
name.

So where AWR is a single update that *is* both roads at high temperature, Muesli is a
single update that *contains* both roads at any temperature — a gradient term that
ascends $J$, and a regression term that pulls toward $\pi^{*}$. The clip is what makes
that combination safe to take in one step: bounding $\hat A$ to $[-c, c]$ bounds the
target's total-variation distance from the prior by $\tanh(c/2)$, independent of the
size of the action space (their Theorem 4.1). It is an automatic trust region, playing
for the EM term the role the clip plays in PPO.

<Aside type="note" title="Why keep the gradient term at all">
	The CMPO regression on its own is already a complete EM-style method. Muesli keeps
	the policy-gradient term beside it because the regression target is built from a
	one-step model value, $\hat q = \hat r_1 + \gamma\, \hat v_1$, while the gradient
	term can carry a multi-step return. The sum lets the stable, bounded EM jump fix the
	destination while the lower-bias policy gradient corrects the step — each road
	supplying what the other lacks.
</Aside>

## A single objective, two trajectories

The cell runs both methods on the one-dimensional Gaussian bandit from the AWR
page. The policy gradient ascends the objective with a score-function estimate;
EM (in its AWR form) jumps to the advantage-weighted mean and settles. They begin
from the same point and reach the same optimum by visibly different paths.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

a_star = 2.0                              # the (unknown) best action
reward = lambda a: -(a - a_star) ** 2     # reward peaks at a_star

def policy_gradient(iters=60, N=64, sigma=0.8, lr=0.05):
    mu, traj = -3.0, [-3.0]
    for _ in range(iters):
        a    = rng.normal(mu, sigma, N)              # sample from current policy
        A    = reward(a) - reward(a).mean()          # advantage vs batch baseline
        grad = (A * (a - mu) / sigma ** 2).mean()    # REINFORCE score-function grad
        mu  += lr * grad                             # an infinitesimal step uphill
        traj.append(mu)
    return np.array(traj)

def em_awr(iters=60, N=64, sigma=0.8, beta=0.5, lr=0.5):
    mu, traj = -3.0, [-3.0]
    for _ in range(iters):
        a       = rng.normal(mu, sigma, N)
        A       = reward(a) - reward(a).mean()
        w       = np.minimum(np.exp(A / beta), 20.0) # exponentiated advantage
        mu_star = (w * a).sum() / w.sum()            # jump to weighted-regression optimum
        mu     += lr * (mu_star - mu)
        traj.append(mu)
    return np.array(traj)

pg, em = policy_gradient(), em_awr()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.6))
ax.plot(pg, label="policy gradient  (ascend)")
ax.plot(em, label="EM / AWR  (jump and imitate)")
ax.axhline(a_star, ls="--", c="k", lw=1, label="shared optimum")
ax.set_xlabel("iteration"); ax.set_ylabel("policy mean  mu")
ax.set_title("two roads, one optimum")
ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
fig.tight_layout()

print("policy gradient  final mu:", round(float(pg[-1]), 3))
print("EM / AWR         final mu:", round(float(em[-1]), 3), "  (target 2.0)")
`}

<Aside type="tip" title="Reading the two paths">
	Both means converge on the best action, because the objective they climb is
	the same. The policy gradient traces the noisier, incremental ascent; EM moves
	in larger jumps and settles sooner. The destination is fixed by the shared
	objective; the trajectory is fixed by the mechanism.
</Aside>

## Which to use

The shared objective means the choice is rarely about *what* is optimized and
almost always about the data regime and the engineering budget.

- **On-policy, fresh rollouts, large-scale infrastructure** favour the policy
  gradient. The clip is simple and parallelizes cleanly; GRPO is the present
  standard for RL post-training of language models.
- **Off-policy, replay reuse, or fully offline data** favour EM. Regressing onto
  buffered actions reuses data, and in the offline case it cannot pursue the
  spurious out-of-distribution actions a critic might otherwise chase, because it
  only ever upweights actions the data already contains.
- **Sample-scarce or expensive environments** favour EM, for the same reuse.
- **Preference data with no reward model** favours the limit of the EM road —
  DPO — which removes the reinforcement-learning loop entirely.

## DPO: the limit of the EM road

When the optimum $\pi^{*} \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$ is known in closed
form, the reward can be recovered from it,
$r = \beta \log \tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} + \beta \log Z$, and substituted
into a preference loss; the reward model and the RL loop both cancel. This is
**Direct Preference Optimization**, the inference road taken to its conclusion. It
has no policy-gradient counterpart: one cannot dispense with the ascent when the
ascent *is* the method. The closed form is what makes the shortcut available, and
the closed form belongs to the EM road.

## The two questions

The track reduces to a single placement test. Confronted with any new
policy-optimization method, ask:

1. **What advantage signal does it use** — Monte-Carlo returns, a learned $Q$, an
   on-policy GAE estimate, a group-relative baseline?
2. **Does it ascend the KL-regularized objective, or regress onto its optimum?**

The first answer locates the method's estimator; the second places it on the
policy-gradient road, the EM road, or — like AWR — on the seam between them. Nearly
every modern method falls into place under these two questions.
