We aim to maximize $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta}[R(\tau)]$ by
gradient ascent, where $R(\tau) = \sum_t \gamma^t r_t$. The difficulty is that
$\theta$ controls the *distribution being averaged over*, not merely the
integrand. The gradient cannot be moved through an expectation whose sampling
distribution depends on $\theta$ without further work.

## The log-derivative trick

The resolution is a single line of algebra that recurs throughout machine
learning. For any distribution $p_\theta(x)$ and function $f$,

$$
\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}[f(x)]
= \int f(x)\, \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)\, dx
= \int f(x)\, p_\theta(x)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\, dx
= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}\!\big[\, f(x)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) \,\big].
$$

The middle step uses $\nabla_\theta p = p\, \nabla_\theta \log p$. The
consequence is that the gradient is *itself an expectation*, so it can be
estimated by sampling, and only $\log p_\theta$ need be differentiated, never
$f$.

Applying this to trajectories, recall that $\log p_\theta(\tau) = \log
\rho_0(s_0) + \sum_t \big[\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) + \log P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t,
a_t)\big]$. The start and transition terms do not depend on $\theta$, so they
**drop out** of the gradient:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t).
$$

This is the point at which RL becomes **model-free**: the dynamics $P$ need never
be known or differentiated. It suffices to sample from the environment and
differentiate the policy itself.

## The policy gradient theorem

Combining the two results gives the **REINFORCE** estimator:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta}\!\left[\, R(\tau) \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \,\right].
$$

This may be read as a weighted vote: each action's $\nabla_\theta \log
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ (the *score* — the direction in parameter space that
makes $a_t$ more likely) is scaled by the total reward the trajectory obtained.
High-reward trajectories make their actions more probable; low-reward
trajectories make theirs less probable.

One immediate refinement follows from the observation that an action at time $t$
cannot influence rewards in the past. Discarding those terms — retaining only the
**reward-to-go** $G_t$ — leaves the gradient unbiased while reducing its
variance:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, G_t \,\right].
$$

## Baselines and the advantage

REINFORCE is correct but exhibits high variance: returns vary considerably from
episode to episode, and the gradient estimate inherits that variability. The most
important variance reduction is to subtract a **baseline** $b(s_t)$ that does not
depend on the action:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \big(G_t - b(s_t)\big) \,\right].
$$

This remains **unbiased** for *any* such $b$, because the score has zero mean
under the policy:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\!\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big]
= \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}
= \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0.
$$

The natural choice is $b(s) = V^\pi(s)$, which converts the weight into the
**advantage** from [the setup](/reinforcement-learning/the-setup/):

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, A^\pi(s_t, a_t) \,\right].
$$

The sign now has a clear interpretation: above-average actions ($A > 0$) are
made more probable, below-average actions ($A < 0$) less probable, and the
magnitude reflects how much better than average the action is, rather than the
size of the raw return.

## Actor-critic and GAE

Since $A^\pi$ is unknown, it must be estimated. A **critic** $V_\phi(s)$ is
learned (by regressing it toward observed returns) alongside the **actor**
$\pi_\theta$. The simplest advantage estimate is the one-step **TD error**:

$$
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t) \;\approx\; A^\pi(s_t, a_t).
$$

Using $\delta_t$ yields low variance but is biased, as it relies on the critic;
using the full return $G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$ is unbiased but has high variance.
**Generalized Advantage Estimation** (GAE) interpolates between them with a
single parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$:

$$
\hat{A}_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, \lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}.
$$

At $\lambda = 0$ it reduces to the one-step TD error; at $\lambda = 1$ it
recovers the full Monte-Carlo advantage. This $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage
estimate that PPO and most modern policy-gradient methods supply to the update.

## The deterministic policy gradient

Everything above differentiates a *stochastic* policy through its log-probability:
$\theta$ enters only through the distribution the action is drawn from, and the score
$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$ is the sole point at which the gradient touches the
policy. A second route applies when the policy is **deterministic**, $a = \mu_\theta(s)$
— a point rather than a distribution. This is the natural form in continuous action
spaces, where the alternative, $\arg\max_a Q(s, a)$, is itself an optimization at every
step.

With no distribution over actions to average, the objective is taken over the discounted
state-visitation distribution $\rho^\mu$ induced by $\mu_\theta$,

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho^\mu}\!\big[\, Q^\mu\big(s, \mu_\theta(s)\big) \,\big],
$$

and its gradient follows from the ordinary chain rule, threading $\theta$ through the
action and into the critic's dependence on it:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho^\mu}\!\Big[\, \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\; \nabla_a Q^\mu(s, a)\big|_{a = \mu_\theta(s)} \,\Big].
$$

This is the **deterministic policy gradient** (Silver et al., 2014), and the contrast
with REINFORCE is structural. There is no score and no expectation over actions; the
update is not a return-weighted vote but a direct push on the action in the direction
$\nabla_a Q$ that the critic reports as improving, backpropagated into the parameters
through $\nabla_\theta \mu_\theta$. The price is that it requires a *differentiable*
critic $Q^\mu$, so it is inherently an actor-critic method: the actor ascends the
critic's action-gradient while the critic is fit to the actor's behavior.

Two consequences follow. Because the state distribution $\rho^\mu$ need not be generated
by the current policy, the deterministic gradient is **off-policy** — states may be
replayed from a buffer filled by an exploratory behavior policy, which the on-policy
score-function estimator cannot do. And removing the action-sampling noise removes a
large source of variance, at the cost of a bias that now resides entirely in the critic.
This is the basis of **DDPG** and, with its value overestimation corrected, **TD3**. It
is not a phenomenon separate from the stochastic gradient but its zero-noise limit: as
the variance of a Gaussian policy is sent to zero, the stochastic policy gradient
converges to the deterministic one.

The remainder of this track follows the stochastic, on-policy line.

## See the baseline work

Consider the score-function estimator on a 10-armed bandit with a `softmax`
policy. Every arm's reward is centered around **+5**. This offset carries no
information about *which* arm is best, since it is common to all of them, yet
without a baseline it multiplies into every gradient estimate as noise. We draw
many one-sample estimates at a fixed policy and examine the best arm's gradient
component, **with and without** a baseline equal to the mean reward:

## The limitation that the next page addresses

Policy gradients carry a subtle fragility. The estimator is valid only *at* the
current $\theta$; it is a local, on-policy quantity. A step that is too large
leads the new policy to visit states the gradient never accounted for, the update
can collapse the policy, and recovery is difficult. Moreover, because each batch
of experience is valid for only one gradient step, sample efficiency is poor.

The next two routes both address the question of how to take a step that is both
large and safe — first by explicitly bounding how far the policy may move
([trust regions](/reinforcement-learning/trust-regions/)), and later by
reframing the problem so that the safe step follows automatically
([RL as inference](/reinforcement-learning/rl-as-inference/)).
