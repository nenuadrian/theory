The policy gradient supplies a *direction*. It says nothing reliable about *how
far* to move. A step that is too large lands the policy in a region its own
gradient never sampled, performance degrades sharply, and — unlike supervised
learning — the next batch of data is generated by the degraded policy, so there
is no clean recovery. Trust-region methods make the step size principled.

## A surrogate you can optimize off-policy

We begin by writing the improvement of a new policy $\pi_\theta$ over the current
$\pi_{\text{old}}$ in terms of the **old** policy's advantage:

$$
J(\pi_\theta) - J(\pi_{\text{old}})
= \mathbb{E}_{s, a \sim \pi_\theta}\!\big[\, A^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s, a) \,\big].
$$

This is exact but not directly usable: the expectation is over $\pi_\theta$, the
very policy being chosen. The remedy is to **sample with the old policy** and
correct with an importance ratio
$\rho_\theta(s,a) = \tfrac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}$,
then approximate the state distribution as unchanged:

$$
L_{\pi_{\text{old}}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{s, a \sim \pi_{\text{old}}}\!\left[\, \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}\, A^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s, a) \,\right].
$$

This **surrogate** can be estimated from the data already collected, and its
gradient at $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$ equals the policy gradient exactly.
The approximation — neglecting the shift in the *state* distribution — is
accurate *only while $\pi_\theta$ remains close to $\pi_{\text{old}}$*. The
objective is therefore to maximize $L$ while constraining the policy from moving
too far.

## TRPO: a hard KL constraint

The foundational result (Kakade & Langford; Schulman et al.) makes this
constraint precise. The true objective is **lower-bounded** by the surrogate
minus a penalty proportional to how far the policy moved, measured in [KL
divergence](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/):

$$
J(\pi_\theta) \;\ge\; L_{\pi_{\text{old}}}(\theta) - C\, \overline{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{old}}, \pi_\theta).
$$

Maximizing the right-hand side *guarantees* that the true objective does not
worsen — **monotonic improvement**. In practice the theoretical $C$ forces very
small steps, so **Trust Region Policy Optimization** (TRPO) recasts the penalty
as a hard constraint with a tunable trust-region size $\delta$:

$$
\max_\theta\; L_{\pi_{\text{old}}}(\theta)
\quad \text{subject to} \quad
\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\big[\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) \,\big] \le \delta.
$$

To solve it, TRPO linearizes the objective and takes a *second-order* view of the
constraint. The local curvature of the KL is the
[**Fisher information matrix**](/divergences/fisher-information/) $F$, so
the step becomes a **natural gradient**:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta + \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^\top F^{-1} g}}\; F^{-1} g,
\qquad g = \nabla_\theta L.
$$

The factor $F^{-1}$ rescales the raw gradient $g$ by the geometry of the policy
distribution. It measures distance in *probability space* rather than parameter
space, so a step of a given size produces the same change in behavior regardless
of how the network is parameterized. This yields strong guarantees, but
computing $F^{-1} g$ (via conjugate gradients) together with a line search to
enforce the constraint is computationally demanding.

<Aside type="note" title="Why the natural gradient at all">
	Two networks may have very different parameters yet represent nearly the
	same policy, or nearly identical parameters yet represent very different
	policies. Plain gradient descent, operating in parameter space, cannot
	distinguish these cases. The Fisher matrix supplies that information, so a
	natural-gradient step changes the *policy* by a controlled amount. This
	controlled change in behavior is precisely what a trust region requires.
</Aside>

## PPO: the same idea, first-order

**Proximal Policy Optimization** (PPO) retains TRPO's goal — preventing the ratio
$\rho_\theta$ from straying too far — but enforces it with an inexpensive,
first-order mechanism that integrates into any SGD loop. The **clipped
surrogate** is

$$
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\, \min\!\Big(\rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t,\;\; \operatorname{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1 - \epsilon,\, 1 + \epsilon\big)\, \hat{A}_t \Big) \,\right],
$$

with $\rho_t(\theta) = \tfrac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}$
and $\hat{A}_t$ the GAE estimate. The mechanism, organized by the sign of the
advantage, is as follows:

- For **$\hat{A}_t > 0$** (a favorable action), the objective rises as $\rho_t$
  increases, but the `clip` caps it at $1 + \epsilon$. Beyond that point,
  additional probability yields *no further* objective, so there is no incentive
  to overshoot.
- For **$\hat{A}_t < 0$** (an unfavorable action), the situation is symmetric.
  *Lowering* $\rho_t$ is rewarded, but only down to $1 - \epsilon$.
- The outer **`min`** makes the clip one-sided in the safe direction: it never
  rewards moving the ratio further out, but it does still penalize a step that
  overshot, so the update can pull an overly aggressive ratio back.

There is no Fisher matrix and no constraint solve, only a clamp on the ratio.
Because the surrogate remains valid over a small region, PPO reuses each batch
for **several epochs** of minibatch SGD, which is far more sample-efficient than
vanilla policy gradients. A penalty variant also exists, replacing the clip with
an explicit $-\beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ term with $\beta$ adapted to meet a target
KL. This variant is worth noting, because that KL penalty is precisely where the
[inference view](/reinforcement-learning/rl-as-inference/) reappears.

<Aside type="tip" title="TRPO vs PPO in one line">
	TRPO bounds the KL **exactly** with a constrained second-order step, giving
	strong guarantees at high computational cost. PPO bounds it **approximately**
	by clipping the ratio, giving weaker guarantees but with sufficient simplicity
	and scalability to become the default for applications ranging from robotics to
	RLHF.
</Aside>

## Where this is heading

PPO is the standard method, but it carries an additional cost: a **critic**
$V_\phi$, typically as large as the policy, trained alongside it solely to
compute $\hat{A}_t$. When the policy is a multi-billion-parameter language model
and the reward arrives only at the *end* of a long generation, that critic is
both expensive and difficult to fit. [GRPO](/reinforcement-learning/grpo/) asks
whether it can be dispensed with entirely.
