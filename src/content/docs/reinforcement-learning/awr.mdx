[MPO](/reinforcement-learning/mpo/) and [V-MPO](/reinforcement-learning/vmpo/)
wrap the [EM loop](/reinforcement-learning/rl-as-inference/) in considerable machinery: a
temperature dual, a decoupled trust region, and learned critics. **Advantage-Weighted
Regression** (AWR, Peng et al. 2019) asks how much of that machinery may be removed. The
answer is that nearly all of it may be removed. What remains is two supervised regressions in a loop.

## The whole algorithm

Maintain a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ of past experience, and repeat the following:

1. **Fit the value** (the baseline) by regressing onto observed returns:

$$
\phi \leftarrow \arg\min_{\phi}\; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{D}}\big[\, \big(R_s - V_\phi(s)\big)^2 \,\big].
$$

2. **Fit the policy** by regressing onto past actions, each weighted by its
   exponentiated advantage:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \arg\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(s, a) \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\, \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,\big(R_{s,a} - V_\phi(s)\big)\Big) \,\right].
$$

That second line is precisely the M-step from the inference page, with the
advantage $A = R - V$ estimated by Monte-Carlo returns. There is **no E-step
dual**, as $\beta$ is a fixed temperature. There is **no explicit trust
region**, and none is required, because the actions regressed
upon were generated by recent policies, so imitating them *automatically* keeps
$\pi_\theta$ near its prior location. The weights $\exp(A/\beta)$ are typically
clipped at a maximum to prevent a few high-return samples from dominating.

AWR is therefore **MPO with the minimal E-step**: a fixed temperature, no
filtering, Monte-Carlo advantages, and the implicit trust region that arises directly
from regressing onto buffered data. It is fully off-policy, notably simple, and
stable.

## Watch it climb

Consider AWR on a one-dimensional continuous bandit: the reward peaks at an unknown
best action $a^\star$, the policy is a Gaussian, and each iteration samples
actions, computes advantages against the batch mean, and refits the mean by
**advantage-weighted regression** — the closed form for a Gaussian's mean being
the weighted average of the sampled actions. We sweep the temperature $\beta$:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

a_star = 2.0                              # the (unknown) best action
reward = lambda a: -(a - a_star) ** 2     # reward peaks at a_star

def awr(beta, iters=60, N=64, sigma=0.8, lr=0.5):
    mu = -3.0                             # start the policy mean far away
    traj = [mu]
    for _ in range(iters):
        a = rng.normal(mu, sigma, N)      # sample actions from current policy
        r = reward(a)
        A = r - r.mean()                  # advantage vs the batch baseline V
        w = np.minimum(np.exp(A / beta), 20.0)   # exp-weight, then clip
        mu_target = (w * a).sum() / w.sum()      # advantage-weighted regression
        mu += lr * (mu_target - mu)              # move the policy toward it
        traj.append(mu)
    return np.array(traj)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.4))
for beta in [2.0, 0.5, 0.1]:
    ax.plot(awr(beta), label=f"beta = {beta}")
ax.axhline(a_star, ls="--", c="k", lw=1, label="best action")
ax.set_xlabel("EM iteration"); ax.set_ylabel("policy mean  mu")
ax.set_title("AWR climbing to the best action")
ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
fig.tight_layout()

for beta in [2.0, 0.5, 0.1]:
    print(f"beta = {beta}:  final mu = {awr(beta)[-1]:.3f}   (target {a_star})")

<Aside type="tip" title="The temperature, one last time">
	A small $\beta$ concentrates the weights on the best sampled actions, so the
	policy moves toward them rapidly but with greater fluctuation. A large $\beta$ spreads the
	weights out, so the policy advances more cautiously. This is the same dial as
	MPO's $\eta$ and the E-step's $\alpha$; AWR holds it fixed rather than
	solving for it.
</Aside>

## AWAC: the off-policy upgrade

AWR estimates advantages from Monte-Carlo returns, which is approximately on-policy and slow
when data is scarce. **AWAC** (Nair et al. 2020) substitutes a learned $Q_\phi$, so that
the advantage $A^\pi(s, a) = Q_\phi(s, a) - \mathbb{E}_{a' \sim \pi}[Q_\phi(s,
a')]$ can be evaluated from purely off-policy data. The policy update is
unchanged in spirit — advantage-weighted regression with weights
$\exp(\tfrac{1}{\lambda} A^\pi(s, a))$ — and this makes AWAC a strong method for
**offline RL** and for **online fine-tuning** of an offline-pretrained policy.

There is a principled reason it suits offline RL. The weighting $\exp(A/\lambda)$ only
ever *upweights actions that appear in the data*; the policy is fit by regression
onto observed actions, never by querying the critic on actions the data never
contained. It therefore cannot pursue the spurious high-$Q$ out-of-distribution actions
that undermine naive offline methods. The KL-to-the-data regularization is **implicit
in the regression itself** — the very same $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(A/
\lambda)$ closed form, now interpreted as a requirement to stay close to the behavior policy.

## The whole family on one page

Every method on the second road is the same loop: *build* $\pi^* \propto
\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(A/\alpha)$, *then imitate it by weighted regression*. They
differ only in the dials:

| Method | Advantage from | Temperature | Data | Extra trust region |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **AWR** | Monte-Carlo $R - V$ | fixed $\beta$ | replay buffer | implicit (regress on buffer) |
| **AWAC** | learned $Q$ | fixed $\lambda$ | offline / replay | implicit |
| **MPO** | learned $Q$ | KL dual $\eta$ | replay buffer | explicit, decoupled |
| **V-MPO** | on-policy $A$, top-half | KL dual $\eta$ | fresh rollouts | explicit, decoupled |

And the deeper point — the reason this track has two halves that keep rhyming:

<Aside type="note" title="Two roads, one objective">
	Every algorithm here optimizes $\;\mathbb{E}_\pi[A] - \alpha\,
	D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$.

	The **policy-gradient road** — REINFORCE, [PPO](/reinforcement-learning/trust-regions/),
	[GRPO](/reinforcement-learning/grpo/) — *ascends* it by estimating
	$\nabla_\theta$, clipping the step, and penalizing the KL.

	The **EM road** — MPO, V-MPO, AWR — *jumps to its closed-form optimum*
	$\pi^{*} \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(A/\alpha)$ and **imitates** it by
	weighted regression.

	The two approaches address the same hill. One side differentiates its way up; the other reads off the summit
	and proceeds to it. To understand both is to understand the structure of nearly every modern
	policy-optimization method.
</Aside>

This is the map — from the policy gradient through GRPO on one side, and from
RL-as-inference through MPO, V-MPO, and AWR on the other. For any new method one
encounters, the two questions this track repeatedly poses suffice: *what advantage signal
does it use, and does it ascend the KL-regularized objective or regress onto its
optimum?* In nearly all cases, that placement follows. Those two questions, and the
full side-by-side comparison, are drawn together on the final page:
[PG vs EM](/reinforcement-learning/pg-vs-em/).
