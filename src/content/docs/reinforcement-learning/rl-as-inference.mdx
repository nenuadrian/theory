The first road treated reinforcement learning as optimization: one writes down
$J(\theta)$, estimates its gradient, and ascends. This page develops the **second
road**, which reframes control as **inference**. The question is no longer which
direction is uphill, but rather, given the requirement to behave optimally, what is
the *probability* that each action should be taken. The reframing is not merely
interpretive: it yields the optimal policy in **closed form**, and that closed form
proves to be precisely what PPO and GRPO had been approximating throughout.

The second road is an instance of a procedure that predates reinforcement learning
by decades: **expectation maximization** (EM). It is worth stating EM first in its
canonical form — the standard remedy for maximum-likelihood estimation when each
observation is accompanied by an unobserved, or *latent*, variable — and only then
specializing it to control. The structure established in the general case — a lower
bound on the objective, an alternation between completing the data and refitting the
model, and a guarantee that the likelihood cannot decrease — transfers intact to
policy optimization.

## The obstacle: a latent variable

Consider a model $p(x, z; \theta)$ over an observed variable $x$ and a latent
variable $z$, with parameters $\theta$. Only $x$ is seen; $z$ must be summed out
to obtain the quantity maximum likelihood maximizes, the **log-evidence**

$$
\log p(x; \theta) \;=\; \log \sum_{z} p(x, z; \theta).
$$

The logarithm of a sum does not separate, so neither a closed form nor a clean
gradient is generally available. The underlying difficulty is circular: the best
parameters depend on which latent value generated each point, and that assignment
depends in turn on the parameters.

The canonical instance is a **mixture of Gaussians**. Each point $x_i$ is drawn
from one of $K$ components, but the component identity $z_i$ is unobserved. Were
the labels known, fitting each component would be elementary; were the parameters
known, the labels would follow from Bayes' rule. EM resolves the circularity by
alternating between the two.

## A lower bound on the evidence

Introduce *any* distribution $q(z)$ over the latent. For every such $q$, the
log-evidence admits an exact decomposition:

$$
\log p(x; \theta) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, z; \theta)}{q(z)}\right]}_{\textstyle \mathcal{L}(q, \theta)} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z \mid x; \theta)\big)}_{\textstyle \ge\, 0}.
$$

The identity follows from $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\,p(x)$ and the fact that
$\log p(x;\theta)$ does not depend on $z$. The first term $\mathcal{L}(q, \theta)$
is the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO); the second is the KL divergence between
$q$ and the true posterior, which is nonnegative. Hence

$$
\mathcal{L}(q, \theta) \;\le\; \log p(x; \theta), \qquad \text{with equality if and only if } q(z) = p(z \mid x; \theta).
$$

Two features of this decomposition drive everything that follows. It is an
**exact identity**, not an approximation. And because its left-hand side is
independent of $q$, any gain in $\mathcal{L}$ at fixed $\theta$ is offset by an
equal reduction in the KL gap — raising the bound tightens it.

<Aside type="note" title="The direction of the gap">
	The gap is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(z \mid x))$, with $q$ in the first
	argument — the [**reverse** KL](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/) that
	variational methods always minimize. EM is the special case in which $q$ is
	unconstrained, so the gap can be driven to *exactly* zero rather than merely
	made small.
</Aside>

## The two steps

EM is coordinate ascent on the single function $\mathcal{L}(q, \theta)$:
maximize it over $q$, then over $\theta$, and repeat.

**E-step (expectation).** Hold $\theta = \theta^{(t)}$ fixed and maximize
$\mathcal{L}$ over $q$. The only $q$-dependent term is the KL gap, so the
maximizer is the value that closes it:

$$
q^{(t)}(z) \;=\; p\big(z \mid x; \theta^{(t)}\big).
$$

The latent is *completed* by its posterior under the current parameters, and the
bound now touches the log-evidence at $\theta^{(t)}$.

**M-step (maximization).** Hold $q = q^{(t)}$ fixed and maximize $\mathcal{L}$
over $\theta$. Discarding the $\theta$-independent entropy $-\mathbb{E}_q[\log q]$,
this is

$$
\theta^{(t+1)} \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{q^{(t)}}\!\big[\log p(x, z; \theta)\big].
$$

The objective is the **expected complete-data log-likelihood** — the likelihood
one could write if the latent were observed, averaged over the posterior just
computed. With the assignment filled in, the maximization is the tractable,
fully-observed one.

## Why it cannot decrease the likelihood

The two steps chain into a monotonicity guarantee. After the E-step the bound is
tight, $\mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) = \log p(x; \theta^{(t)})$. The M-step
raises the bound, $\mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)})$.
And the bound lies below the evidence everywhere. Concatenating,

$$
\log p(x; \theta^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \mathcal{L}(q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) \;=\; \log p(x; \theta^{(t)}).
$$

The likelihood is **nondecreasing at every iteration**. EM never takes a backward
step — a guarantee no plain gradient ascent on $\log p$ provides without a
line search. It converges to a stationary point of the likelihood, though not
necessarily the global maximum; the bound is a *minorizer* that touches the
objective at the current iterate, which makes EM the prototypical
minorize-maximize scheme.

## EM on a Gaussian mixture

The mixture model makes each step concrete. The E-step assigns each point a
**responsibility** — the posterior probability that it belongs to each component.
The M-step refits each component's mean and variance as a responsibility-weighted
average, and the mixing weights as the average responsibility. The cell fits two
one-dimensional Gaussians to unlabelled data and records the log-likelihood at
every iteration.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Ground-truth two-component mixture. EM observes x only, never the labels z.
n = 400
z = rng.random(n) < 0.35
x = np.where(z, rng.normal(6.0, 1.0, n), rng.normal(1.0, 1.5, n))

def normal_pdf(x, mu, var):
    return np.exp(-(x - mu) ** 2 / (2 * var)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)

# Initialize deliberately away from the truth.
mu  = np.array([0.0, 3.0])
var = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
pi  = np.array([0.5, 0.5])

loglik = []
for step in range(30):
    # E-step: responsibilities r[k, i] = p(z = k | x_i) under current params.
    comp  = np.stack([pi[k] * normal_pdf(x, mu[k], var[k]) for k in range(2)])
    total = comp.sum(0)
    r     = comp / total
    loglik.append(np.log(total).sum())
    # M-step: maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood (closed form).
    Nk  = r.sum(1)
    pi  = Nk / n
    mu  = (r * x).sum(1) / Nk
    var = (r * (x - mu[:, None]) ** 2).sum(1) / Nk

fig, (axL, axR) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8.6, 3.2))
grid = np.linspace(x.min() - 1, x.max() + 1, 300)
axL.hist(x, bins=40, density=True, color="lightgray")
for k in range(2):
    axL.plot(grid, pi[k] * normal_pdf(grid, mu[k], var[k]), lw=2)
axL.set_title("data and the two fitted components")
axR.plot(loglik, marker="o", ms=3, color="teal")
axR.set_title("data log-likelihood per EM step")
axR.set_xlabel("iteration")
fig.tight_layout()

order = np.argsort(mu)
print("recovered means:    ", np.round(mu[order], 2), "  (truth 1.0, 6.0)")
print("recovered weights:  ", np.round(pi[order], 2))
print("monotone increasing:", bool(np.all(np.diff(loglik) >= -1e-9)))

	The responsibilities are the E-step's posterior — soft, fractional labels
	rather than hard assignments. The weighted moments are the M-step, the
	closed-form maximizer of the expected complete-data likelihood. The

	step and never falls.

## The same skeleton, applied to control

The remainder of this page retains every component of the procedure above and
changes only its interpretation. The correspondence is exact:

| General EM | Control as inference |
| --- | --- |
| observed variable $x$ | the event that behaviour was optimal, $\mathcal{O} = 1$ |
| latent variable $z$ | the action $a$ |
| posterior $p(z \mid x)$ | the optimal policy $\pi^{*} \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(A/\alpha)$ |
| expected complete-data log-likelihood | advantage-weighted log-likelihood |
| monotone $\log p(x)$ | monotone policy improvement |

The E-step will compute a posterior over actions — a reweighted policy $\pi^{*}$ —
and the M-step will fit the network to it. One distinction is worth flagging in
advance. In the mixture model the M-step maximizes the expected complete-data
likelihood directly; in control the target $\pi^{*}$ is *nonparametric* and must be
**projected** onto the network by minimizing the forward KL
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$ — the
[direction maximum likelihood minimizes](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/). That
projection is itself a weighted maximum likelihood, so the two coincide in form.

## Optimality as a random variable

Attach to every step a binary **optimality** variable $\mathcal{O}_t$, with

$$
p(\mathcal{O}_t = 1 \mid s_t, a_t) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(r(s_t, a_t) / \alpha\big).
$$

The event "$\mathcal{O}_t = 1$" denotes that *this step was optimal*, and high-reward steps are
exponentially more likely to be so flagged. Conditioning the trajectory on being
optimal throughout, we ask for the posterior over actions, $p(\tau \mid
\mathcal{O}_{0:T} = 1)$. Planning thereby becomes **posterior inference** in a graphical
model: which actions would have been taken, given that the outcome was optimal throughout.

This posterior cannot be computed exactly, so we approximate it with a variational
policy $\pi$ and maximize the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO) on $\log p(\mathcal{O}
= 1)$ — exactly the bound built above, now over actions rather than mixture labels.
Carrying out the algebra, the ELBO is — up to constants — exactly a
**KL-regularized return**:

$$
J(\pi) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\big[\, Q(s, a) \,\big]}_{\text{collect reward}}
\;-\; \alpha\, \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid s)\big)}_{\text{stay near the prior}}.
$$

The temperature $\alpha$ trades the two terms off. The reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is
the prior over actions; taken uniform, the KL becomes an **entropy** bonus,
recovering maximum-entropy RL. Taken as the previous policy, it recovers — by
derivation rather than assumption — the KL tether from
[PPO and GRPO](/reinforcement-learning/grpo/). Observe the direction: the
expectation in this KL is taken under $\pi$ itself, making it the
[**reverse** KL](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/), the same direction that
variational inference and the ELBO always minimize.

## The E-step: the optimal policy, in closed form

Consider maximizing $J(\pi)$ over the policy **nonparametrically** —
treating $\pi(\cdot \mid s)$ as a free probability vector on the simplex, with no
network intervening. This is a concave problem with one constraint
($\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = 1$); a Lagrange multiplier yields the maximizer directly:

$$
\boxed{\;\pi^{*}(a \mid s) = \frac{1}{Z(s)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid s)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{Q(s, a)}{\alpha}\Big)\;}
\qquad Z(s) = \sum_{a} \pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid s)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{Q(s, a)}{\alpha}\Big).
$$

This formula warrants careful reading, for the entire second road is contained within it.
The optimal policy is the **prior, reweighted by exponentiated value**. Actions
that the reference already favored *and* that have high $Q$ are amplified; all
others are suppressed. The temperature $\alpha$ governs the degree of amplification: as
$\alpha \to 0$ it collapses onto the single best action (greedy), and as $\alpha \to
\infty$ it relaxes back to the prior $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (no improvement). Since
$\alpha \log Z(s)$ is the **soft value** $V(s)$, one may equivalently write
$\pi^*(a \mid s) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid s)\exp(A(s,a)/\alpha)$, expressed in terms of the
exponentiated **advantage**.

This is the E-step in the control setting: the posterior over actions, $\pi^*$,
written in closed form. It constitutes policy improvement — starting from
$\pi_{\text{ref}}$, it produces a strictly better policy, and it does so without
touching $\theta$.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# A prior policy over 6 actions, plus an advantage for each action.
acts  = np.arange(6)
prior = np.array([0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.13, 0.08, 0.04])   # pi_ref

    return w / w.sum()

print(f"highest-advantage action: {best}")

<Aside type="tip" title="What the temperature does">
	Observe the competition between prior and advantage. At $\beta = 3$ the prior
	still prevails — the peak of $q$ sits on the prior's favored action, only gently
	displaced. As $\beta$ shrinks, the highest-advantage action overtakes it, until at
	$\beta = 0.3$ the mass concentrates on the best action regardless of how unlikely the
	prior had judged it. The temperature is a continuous dial between **imitating
	the prior** and **acting greedily** — the same dial $\alpha$, $\beta$, or $\eta$
	that every method on this road exposes under a different name.

## The M-step: imitate it with the network

A difficulty remains: $\pi^*$ is *nonparametric*. It is defined pointwise from $Q$ and the
prior, not as a deployable neural network. We therefore **project** it back onto the
parametric family $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the *forward* KL
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$ — the expectation taken under the
fixed target $\pi^{*}$, which is precisely the
[direction maximum likelihood minimizes](/divergences/kl-forward-reverse/).
Expanding it yields plain **weighted maximum likelihood**:

$$
\min_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{s}\!\big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi^{*}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) \big]
\;\;\equiv\;\;
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{s,\, a \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}\!\left[\, \underbrace{\exp\!\Big(\frac{A(s, a)}{\alpha}\Big)}_{\text{weight}}\; \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \,\right].
$$

This is a supervised-learning objective, and the exact control analogue of the
mixture M-step: the network is regressed onto observed actions, each weighted by
its exponentiated advantage. Favorable actions (large $A$) receive large weights and
are strongly imitated; unfavorable actions receive vanishing weights and are
effectively ignored.

The two steps form an **expectation-maximization** loop:

1. **E-step** — given the current value estimates, construct the improved
   nonparametric target $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(A/\alpha)$.
2. **M-step** — fit $\pi_\theta$ to $\pi^*$ by advantage-weighted regression.
3. Set $\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$, refresh the value estimates, and
   repeat.

It is the same alternation proved monotone above, now improving the policy rather
than the data likelihood.

<Aside type="note" title="The two roads, now visibly the same">
	Both roads optimize $\mathbb{E}[A] - \alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \,\|\,
	\pi_{\text{ref}})$. PPO and GRPO **ascend** it with a clipped policy gradient and a
	KL penalty. The EM road **jumps to its closed-form optimum** $\pi^*$ and then
	**imitates** it — with no policy gradient and no clipping, by weighted regression alone.
	The objective and the optimum are the same; one approach differentiates toward it, the other
	regresses onto it.
</Aside>

<Aside type="tip" title="And this is why DPO exists">
	In RLHF the same closed form reads $\pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y
	\mid x)\exp(r(x, y)/\beta)$. Inverting it for the reward, $r = \beta \log
	\tfrac{\pi^*}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} + \beta \log Z$, and substituting into a preference
	loss, both the reward model and the RL loop cancel out — this is **Direct
	Preference Optimization**. It is the inference view taken to its conclusion: when
	the optimum is known in closed form, RL may be dispensed with entirely.
</Aside>

execute each step: [MPO](/reinforcement-learning/mpo/) (off-policy, learned $Q$,
